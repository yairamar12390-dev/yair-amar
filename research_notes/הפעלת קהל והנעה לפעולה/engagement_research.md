# Audience Engagement and Activation Inside a Lecture/Talk: Research Evidence

Research date: 2026-11-17. Evidence grades: STRONG / MODERATE / WEAK / MYTH.
Note on scope of evidence: almost all controlled research comes from university classrooms (mostly STEM, mostly 50-75 min classes, repeated over a semester) or lab video lectures. Transfer to one-off keynotes (no exams, mixed audiences, 20-60 min) is an inference, flagged as such.
Network note: epale.ec.europa.eu (full text of Bradbury 2016) was unreachable in this session; Bradbury details come from PubMed and search summaries.

## 1. Active vs passive listening (active learning, peer instruction, think-pair-share, polling, large audiences)

### Takeaway
Active learning beats pure lecturing with strong, replicated meta-analytic evidence (about +0.47 SD on exams; failure rates 21.8% vs 33.8%), and the best-supported format for big rooms is the "question -> individual vote -> peer discussion -> revote" cycle (Mazur's Peer Instruction). Audiences tend to *feel* they learned less in active formats even when they learned more, so a speaker should expect, and frame, that discomfort. Grade: STRONG (for classrooms); MODERATE when applied to one-off talks.

### Cited Findings
- Freeman, Eddy, McDonough, Smith, Okoroafor, Jordt & Wenderoth (University of Washington), PNAS 2014, 111(23): meta-analysis of 225 studies of undergraduate STEM courses comparing active learning with traditional lecturing. Exam/concept-inventory scores rose by 0.47 SD under active learning (n = 158 studies). Average failure rates were 21.8% with active learning vs 33.8% with lecturing (odds ratio 1.95, risk ratio 1.5). Lecture students were 1.5 times as likely to fail, which is the "55% higher failure rate" headline. Active learning was defined broadly: clicker questions, calling on groups or individuals at random, students explaining concepts to each other. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060683); [Campus Technology summary](https://campustechnology.com/articles/2014/05/14/55-more-stem-students-fail-lectures-than-courses-with-active-learning.aspx)
- Theobald et al. (U. Washington, with Freeman), PNAS 2020: meta-analysis of exam data from 15 studies (9,238 students) and failure-rate data from 26 studies (44,606 students). Active learning shrank achievement gaps for underrepresented students by 33% on exams and 45% on pass rates. Only **high-intensity** courses produced this (at least two-thirds of class time spent active): there the gaps shrank by 42% on exams and 76% on pass rates. Proposed "heads-and-hearts" hypothesis: gains need deliberate practice *plus* inclusive teaching. — [UW Biology](https://biology.washington.edu/research/publications/active-learning-narrows-achievement-gaps-underrepresented-students-0); [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/32152114/)
- Crouch & Mazur (Harvard), American Journal of Physics 2001, 69(9): ten years of Peer Instruction (PI) in introductory physics. Normalized gain on the Force Concept Inventory was about 0.25 under traditional lecture and 0.49 in the first PI semester. It kept rising (1993-1997) as the method was refined. Gains were "regularly twice as large" as under traditional lecture. — [Mazur group page](https://mazur.harvard.edu/publications/peer-instruction-ten-years-experience-and-results); [Derek Bruff summary](https://derekbruff.org/2009/10/23/article-crouch-mazur-2001); [Vickrey et al. review, CBE-LSE](https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.14-11-0198)
- Hake's benchmark (6,000+ students), as reported by PhysPort: average normalized gain was about 23% for traditional lecture (range 13-29%) and about 48% for interactive engagement (range 21-69%). — [PhysPort](https://physport.org/recommendations/Entry.cfm?ID=93336)
- Smith, Wood, Adams, Wieman, Knight, Guild & Su (U. Colorado), Science 2009, 323: peer discussion produces real understanding, not just copying. After discussing, students answered a *new* isomorphic question on their own. On hard questions: 16% were right individually, 32% after discussion, and 54% on the new question answered alone. Gains held even when no one in the group initially knew the answer. Limitation: conceptual questions only. — [Derek Bruff summary](https://derekbruff.org/2009/01/08/article-smith-et-al-2009); [CU VIVO record](https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_30275)
- Hunsu, Adesope & Bayly (Washington State U.), Computers & Education 2016, 94: meta-analysis of audience response systems (clickers): 53 studies, 111 effect sizes, more than 26,000 participants. Effect on cognitive outcomes was **small but significant**; effect on non-cognitive outcomes (engagement, self-efficacy, attendance) was **near-medium**. Moderated by knowledge domain, **class size** and how the questions were used. A secondary summary says gains were largest for higher-order outcomes (application, critical thinking), with no effect on lower-order retention. — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-the-effects-of/99900601053501842); [Southampton digital learning summary](https://generic.wordpress.soton.ac.uk/digital-learning/technologies/student-response-systems-research-tells-us)
- Deslauriers, McCarty, Miller, Callaghan & Kestin (Harvard), PNAS 2019, 116(39): randomized, controlled comparison in large intro physics classes with identical content. Active-class students scored higher on tests but *rated* their learning lower. The authors attribute this to higher cognitive effort, while fluent lectures create an illusion of learning. They warn that relying on perceived learning (for example evaluations) can favor passive methods, and recommend explaining the value of active learning early. — [Full text PDF](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/gamelab/files/2023/01/Deslauriersa-et-al-2019.pdf); [UNL news](https://newsroom.unl.edu/announce/csmce/10018/58996)

### Inferences
- For large audiences, the scalable unit is: pose a conceptual question, give silent think time, collect votes (hands, cards, phones), then "turn to your neighbor and convince them", then revote and explain. Each step is backed by Mazur, Smith et al. and Hunsu et al.
- Intensity matters (Theobald). One token poll in 45 minutes is probably not "active learning" in the sense measured. Classroom effects came from sustained activity.
- The Deslauriers finding implies that speakers who chase applause and "smoothness" ratings may be optimizing for the wrong signal. Brief meta-framing ("this will feel harder, that is the point") is evidence-supported.
- Class size moderates clicker effects (Hunsu). The direction was not retrieved, so do not claim it scales identically to 1,000+ rooms.

### Gaps
- No controlled studies found on active learning in one-off keynotes or conference talks with non-student adults. All effect sizes are from courses that run over weeks.
- Exact Hedges' g values from Hunsu et al. 2016 were not retrieved, nor was the direction of the class-size moderator.
- Think-pair-share as a named technique (Frank Lyman, U. Maryland, 1981) has no dedicated meta-analysis in my results. Its support is indirect via peer-discussion studies (Smith 2009). Grade: MODERATE by extension.

## 2. Attention: the "10-minute attention span", mind-wandering, and resetting attention

### Takeaway
The fixed "attention drops after 10-15 minutes" rule is a **MYTH** as stated: it traces to weak or indirect 1970s sources. What is supported is that attention fluctuates in short lapses throughout a talk, that lapses grow more frequent as a passive talk goes on, that mind-wandering hurts memory, and that inserting questions, tests or demonstrations reduces lapses. Grade for "resets help": MODERATE-STRONG. Grade for any precise interval: WEAK.

### Cited Findings
- Bradbury (Rosalind Franklin University), "Attention span during lectures: 8 seconds, 10 minutes, or more?", Advances in Physiology Education 2016, 40:509-513 (Personal View). The most often cited source for a rapid drop in student attention "barely discusses student attention at all". The 10-15 minute figure propagates from a single 1978 manuscript. The "8 seconds" figure comes from a 2015 Microsoft report covered by Time and is treated as pop myth. Bradbury argues that attention depends more on the teacher and the material than on a fixed clock. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/); [Physiological Society](https://www.physoc.org/magazine-articles/physiology-education-manuscripts-in-demand/)
- Wilson & Korn (Saint Louis University), "Attention During Lectures: Beyond Ten Minutes", Teaching of Psychology 2007, 34(2):85-89. Traced the 10-15 minute claim and found that the underlying research measured attention only indirectly or not at all. Several sources cited for it were not empirical (personal observation, secondary sources). Note-taking volume declined across a lecture, but retention did not. Recommendation: work on motivation rather than a timer. — [Faculty Focus summary](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/instructional-design/student-attention-spans); [Mirjam Glessmer summary](https://mirjamglessmer.com/2014/10/01/development-of-student-attention-over-time/); [typeset PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/attention-during-lectures-beyond-ten-minutes-4zdlj831oy.pdf)
- Bunce, Flens & Neiles (Catholic University of America), Journal of Chemical Education 2010, 87(12):1438-1443. Students self-reported attention lapses with clickers in three chemistry courses. The first lapse spike came about 30 seconds into a segment ("settling in"). Later spikes came at roughly 4.5-5.5, 7-9 and 9-10 minutes; a secondary source gives different timings, so these need checking. Lapses grew more frequent, about every 2 minutes by the end of class. Most lapses lasted 1 minute or less. **Lapses were fewer during demonstrations and clicker questions, and in the lecture segment immediately after them.** — [PDF](https://wikicap.ulb.be/images/5/57/Bunce_Flens_et_al_2010.pdf); [UIW teaching center](https://my.uiw.edu/tlt/center-for-teaching-learning/_docs/designing-interactive-lectures-that-make-the-most-of-students-attention-spans.pdf)
- Risko, Anderson, Sarwal, Engelhardt & Kingstone (U. British Columbia), Applied Cognitive Psychology 2012, 26:234-242, "Everyday Attention: Variation in Mind Wandering and Memory in a Lecture". In a video lecture (about 1 hour), mind-wandering rose with time on task and memory for the material fell. Mind-wandering was significantly related to poorer memory. — [Frontiers citation context](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00619/full); [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1814)
- Conflicting evidence: Wammes, Boucher, Seli, Cheyne & Smilek (U. Waterloo) 2016 found that in a **live** lecture, undergraduates' mind-wandering rates stayed roughly stable across the session, unlike the rising pattern seen in lab video lectures. — [Cognitive Research review, PMC6246756](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6246756)
- Seli, Risko & Smilek 2016 (113 students): an easy, boring task produced more *intentional* mind-wandering; a hard task produced more *unintentional* mind-wandering. Overall rates were similar. This implies that boredom and overload are different failure modes. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/03/160330085617.htm); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=122237)
- Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (Harvard), PNAS 2013, 110(16):6313-6317. Brief memory tests inserted between segments of a video lecture **cut mind-wandering from about 40% to about 20% of probes**, increased note-taking and improved final learning. Caveat from Schacter: a single lecture and topic, and live-class generalization was unclear. Jing, Szpunar & Schacter 2016 found tests improved integration of information across a lecture, though mind-wandering rates did not differ in that study. — [PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699); [Harvard Psychology](https://psychology.fas.harvard.edu/publications/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-online); [MedicalResearch.com interview](https://medicalresearch.com/?p=546); [Jing et al. 2016 PDF](https://sites.harvard.edu/schacter-memory/files/2022/08/jing2016-1-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- Replace "change something every 10 minutes" with "build in a state change roughly every 5-15 minutes". The best-evidenced resets are a question the audience must answer (Bunce; Szpunar) and a demonstration (Bunce).
- The first 30 seconds of any new segment is a vulnerable "settling-in" moment, so open each segment with a hook rather than a throat-clear.
- Two different fixes for two failure modes (Seli et al.): if content is too easy, raise the challenge or stakes; if it is too hard, slow down or chunk.
- Live audiences may wander less over time than video viewers (Wammes 2016). A live speaker's presence may itself be protective. This is a hypothesis, not a firm finding.

### Gaps
- No controlled evidence found on stories, humor, physical movement on stage or vocal variety as attention resets in lectures. These are widely recommended but were not supported by sources retrieved here. Grade: WEAK / unverified.
- Hartley & Davies 1978 and Johnstone & Percival 1976 (the sources often blamed for the 10-minute myth) were not retrieved directly.
- The exact Bunce et al. later spike timings are contested between secondary sources.

## 3. Questions as tools (rhetorical vs real questions, show of hands, curiosity gap, retrieval practice, prediction)

### Takeaway
Questions the audience must actually answer, and ideally commit to, are well supported. Retrieval practice and the testing effect are STRONG; prediction-before-reveal is MODERATE (it works best when the answer surprises); curiosity-gap openers are MODERATE (neural and memory evidence from trivia, not lectures). Rhetorical questions without a commitment step have no evidence found here.

### Cited Findings
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (Washington State U. / Simon Fraser U.), Review of Educational Research 2017, 87(3):659-701. Meta-analysis of practice testing: 272 independent effect sizes (sources disagree on 118 vs 188 articles; about 15,472 participants). Practice tests beat restudying and all other comparison conditions, with a "moderately large" effect. Multiple-choice and mixed formats produced larger effects. Feedback's added benefit was not statistically significant. The exact g was not retrieved. — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842); [WSU PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)
- Szpunar et al. 2013 (see section 2): interpolated retrieval during a lecture halves mind-wandering, so retrieval works as an attention tool as well as a memory tool. — [PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699)
- Kang, Hsu, Krajbich, Loewenstein, McClure, Wang & Camerer (Caltech / Carnegie Mellon), Psychological Science 2009, 20(8):963-973, "The Wick in the Candle of Learning". fMRI study with 19 participants and 40 trivia questions. Curiosity activated reward-anticipation regions (caudate) and people paid scarce tokens or waiting time to learn answers. Higher curiosity predicted better recall of surprising answers 1-2 weeks later. The study builds on Loewenstein's information-gap theory of curiosity. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/); [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf)
- Brod, Hasselhorn & Bunge (DIPF Frankfurt / UC Berkeley), Learning and Instruction 2018, 55:22-31. Generating a prediction before seeing an outcome boosted learning **when the outcome violated the prediction**. The strength of the surprise reaction (measured by eye-tracking and pupil response) correlated with the amount learned. Follow-up: Brod et al. 2022, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, on explicit predictions before numerical facts; Brod et al. 2019, npj Science of Learning, "Lighting the wick in the candle of learning". — [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3677215/download); [Springer 2022](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-021-01904-1); [peDOCS npj 2019](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2022/25224/pdf/NPJ_2019_4_Brod_et_al_Lighting_the_wick_in_the_candle_of_learning_A.pdf)
- Peer Instruction protocol (Crouch & Mazur 2001; Smith et al. 2009): committing to an individual answer *before* discussion is the core mechanism. — [Mazur](https://mazur.harvard.edu/publications/peer-instruction-ten-years-experience-and-results)

### Inferences
- Best-evidenced question sequence for a talk: (1) open a curiosity gap with a counterintuitive question; (2) ask the audience to predict or commit (hands, phone vote, writing a number down); (3) reveal, ideally a surprise; (4) later in the talk, ask them to retrieve the key point without slides ("what were the three...?").
- A show of hands works as a low-cost commitment device but is public. Simultaneous signals (cards, fingers on chest, anonymous polls) reduce conformity and anxiety. This is an inference from the Cooper et al. anxiety findings and Mazur's move from flashcards to clickers.
- Rhetorical questions likely work mainly when they open a genuine information gap. No direct evidence was found.

### Gaps
- No study found directly comparing rhetorical vs answered questions in live talks.
- George Loewenstein, "The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation", Psychological Bulletin 1994 (information-gap theory) was not fetched directly; it is cited here via Kang et al. 2009.
- Roediger & Karpicke 2006 (Washington University in St. Louis, testing effect) was not retrieved in this session, though it is widely cited; Adesope 2017 covers the meta-analytic case.
- Exact effect sizes (g) were not retrieved for Adesope 2017.

## 4. Participation formats (volunteers, demonstrations, small groups, writing/reflection, call-and-response, gamification)

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced formats are pair/small-group discussion of a question (STRONG in classrooms), live demonstrations (MODERATE: fewer attention lapses, per Bunce 2010) and brief writing or retrieval moments (STRONG by way of testing and generation research). On-stage volunteers, call-and-response and gamification inside a single talk have little or no direct research retrieved here (WEAK / no evidence found).

### Cited Findings
- Peer discussion in pairs or small groups improves conceptual understanding beyond what any member initially knew (Smith et al. 2009, Science), and doubles conceptual gains when built into lectures (Crouch & Mazur 2001). — [Bruff on Smith 2009](https://derekbruff.org/2009/01/08/article-smith-et-al-2009); [Mazur](https://mazur.harvard.edu/publications/peer-instruction-ten-years-experience-and-results)
- Demonstrations: fewer self-reported attention lapses during demos and in the segment right after them (Bunce, Flens & Neiles 2010). — [PDF](https://wikicap.ulb.be/images/5/57/Bunce_Flens_et_al_2010.pdf)
- Writing/generating: generating information (rather than reading it) gives about a 0.40 SD memory benefit across 86 studies and 445 effect sizes (Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott & McDaniel 2007, Memory & Cognition). McCurdy et al. 2020 (Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 126 articles, 310 experiments) found larger mean effects; both meta-analyses found free recall gave the smallest effect. — [PubMed Bertsch](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/); [Bertsch PDF](https://mcdaniel97.github.io/Publications/Bertsch%20et%20al.%202007.pdf); [McCurdy 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-020-01762-3)
- Clicker/polling technology: small but significant cognitive gains and near-medium gains in engagement and affect (Hunsu et al. 2016). — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-the-effects-of/99900601053501842)

### Inferences
- Within a keynote: a 60-90 second "turn to your neighbor" or a 30-second "write one sentence" is the highest-evidence, lowest-logistics activation that scales to any room size.
- Volunteers on stage chiefly engage the volunteer plus vicarious attention from others. Without evidence, treat this as a showmanship tool with anxiety risk (see section 6), and use only willing volunteers.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence retrieved on stage volunteers, call-and-response or chanting in lectures. Grade: no evidence found.
- Gamification: a meta-analysis exists (Sailer & Homner 2020, Educational Psychology Review, reportedly small-to-medium positive effects), but it was not retrieved or verified this session; it also concerns courses, not single talks.
- Storytelling/narrative memory advantages in lectures were not researched here (likely covered by another researcher).

## 5. Memory of talks (generation, self-reference, elaboration, retrieval)

### Takeaway
Audiences remember what they themselves generate, retrieve, predict or connect to themselves. Generation effect (about d = 0.40) and testing effect are STRONG. The self-reference effect is MODERATE-STRONG (significant on average, about 0.45, but inconsistent across studies).

### Cited Findings
- Generation effect: Bertsch et al. 2007, overall about 0.40 SD benefit; McCurdy et al. 2020, larger mean effects. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/); [McCurdy 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-020-01762-3)
- Self-reference effect: Symons & Johnson (U. Connecticut / Syracuse), Psychological Bulletin 1997, 121(3):371-394, a meta-analysis of 129 studies. Relating material to oneself beats semantic encoding and other-reference. The average effect size is reported as about .45 by a secondary source (metric unverified). The effect was significant but inconsistent across studies. — [UConn repository](https://digitalcommons.lib.uconn.edu/chip_docs/9); [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/194456453.pdf); [Crump lab note](https://www.crumplab.com/self-reference)
- Testing effect: Adesope et al. 2017 (above). Interpolated testing also improved integration across a lecture (Jing, Szpunar & Schacter 2016). — [WSU PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/); [Jing 2016](https://sites.harvard.edu/schacter-memory/files/2022/08/jing2016-1-1.pdf)
- Curiosity improves memory 1-2 weeks later (Kang et al. 2009). Surprise after prediction improves learning (Brod et al. 2018). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/); [Leiden](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3677215/download)
- Mind-wandering reduces memory for lecture content (Risko et al. 2012). — [Zendy](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1814)

### Inferences
- Concrete moves: "Think of a time when you..." (self-reference); "Before I show you, write your guess" (prediction plus generation); "Tell your neighbor the one idea you will use Monday" (generation plus retrieval plus implementation intention); a final unprompted recall of the talk's 3 points (retrieval).
- Application or behavior change after a talk was not measured in these studies. They measure recall or exams.

### Gaps
- Evidence linking these effects to *applying* talk content (behavior change) was not found. Implementation-intention research (Gollwitzer) may be relevant but was not researched here.
- Elaboration/"elaborative interrogation" meta-analyses (for example Dunlosky et al. 2013, rated moderate utility) were not retrieved this session.

## 6. Pitfalls: when interaction backfires

### Takeaway
The main documented risk is **fear of negative evaluation**: cold calling and forced public answers raise anxiety, and some students would skip class to avoid it. Anonymity, pair-first discussion and community-building mitigate this. Cultural silence is real but overgeneralized. Time loss is a practical risk with little direct research. Grade: MODERATE (anxiety); WEAK-MODERATE (culture).

### Cited Findings
- Cooper, Downing & Brownell (Arizona State U.), International Journal of STEM Education 2018, 5:23. Interview study in large college biology. **Fear of negative evaluation** was the main driver of anxiety in active learning. Cold call or random call increased anxiety; a later paper cites 60% of interviewees saying so (secondary figure). Some students said the thought of being called on made them consider skipping class. Active learning *decreased* anxiety when students felt in control and saw activities building their knowledge. Peer community reduced the fear of negative evaluation. Random call does increase equity of participation, which is a trade-off. — [SpringerOpen PDF](https://stemeducationjournal.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40594-018-0123-6); [UW STEP guide](https://depts.washington.edu/stepuw/managing_anxiety); [SLU guide](https://mha.slu.edu/cttl/resources/resource-guides/reducing-student-anxiety-during-active-learning.pdf)
- Deslauriers et al. 2019: audiences may rate active sessions as less effective, a reputational and feedback risk for the speaker. — [PDF](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/gamelab/files/2023/01/Deslauriersa-et-al-2019.pdf)
- Culture: a 2025 Frontiers in Psychology study (913 Chinese and 189 Korean undergraduates) treats classroom silence as an adaptive form of participation. Chinese students' silence was tied mainly to hierarchical, teacher-centered norms; Korean students' silence more to gender and peer expectations. Other work cautions against a monolithic "Confucian heritage" explanation and finds teaching style and peer behavior shape participation. — [PMC12713118](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12713118/); [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1674145/full); [HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/247611/1/Content.pdf?accept=1)
- Theobald et al. 2020: benefits for underrepresented groups depended on inclusive teaching ("hearts") as well as practice ("heads"). Poorly run activity may not deliver. — [UW Biology](https://biology.washington.edu/research/publications/active-learning-narrows-achievement-gaps-underrepresented-students-0)

### Inferences
- Rules of thumb supported by the above: prefer anonymous or simultaneous responses over singling people out; pair discussion before any public sharing ("what did your partner say?" shifts exposure off the speaker); voluntary volunteers only; explain why you are doing the activity.
- In hierarchical-culture audiences, anonymous polling and pair talk are likely to work better than open-floor questions (inference).
- Time control: use timed, closed-ended activities (vote, 60-second pair talk) in big rooms. Open-ended plenary discussion scales poorly. This is practitioner inference; no research found.

### Gaps
- No empirical studies found on time overruns or loss of control from interaction in talks.
- No studies found on forced participation anxiety specifically in professional or conference (non-student) audiences.
- The 60% cold-call anxiety figure is secondhand; verify in the original paper before citing a number.
