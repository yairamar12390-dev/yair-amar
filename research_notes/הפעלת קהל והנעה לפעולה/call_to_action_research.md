# From Inspiration to Action: The Science of Calls to Action and Behavior Change from a Talk

Researcher notes, current as of November 2026. Evidence grades: **A** = multiple meta-analyses / large preregistered field trials; **B** = one meta-analysis or several solid experiments, with caveats; **C** = single studies, practitioner frameworks, or mixed evidence; **D** = expert opinion or anecdote only.
Items marked **[unverified this session]** come from the researcher's prior knowledge of the literature. They were NOT re-confirmed against a source in this session. The report writer should treat them as leads or cite them cautiously.
Network note: informahealthcare.com and carlsonschool-d8.prd.umn.edu were blocked in this session, so the full text of Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2024) and Goldstein et al. (2008) was not read directly.

## 1. The intention–behavior gap and implementation intentions (if-then plans)

### Takeaway
Moving people emotionally changes intentions much more than it changes behavior. A big shift in intention produces only about half as big a shift in behavior. The best-supported fix to build into a talk's close is the implementation intention, a specific "If situation X, then I will do Y" plan. The original 2006 estimate of its effect was medium-to-large (d = 0.65). The 2024 bias-corrected estimates are much smaller but still positive. Evidence grade: A for the gap, A-/B+ for implementation intentions, with the effect size disputed.

### Cited Findings
- Webb & Sheeran (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* (University of Sheffield). A meta-analysis of 47 experiments that manipulated intention found that a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produced only a small-to-medium change in behavior (d = 0.36). It is the core causal evidence for the gap. — [PubMed 16536643](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/); [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/1580)
- Sheeran & Webb (2016) restate this as d+ = .36 and note that statistical simulations agree: changing intentions does not guarantee behavior change. — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/107519/3/The)
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* vol. 38, pp. 69–119 (NYU / University of Konstanz; University of Sheffield). Across 94 independent tests (reported as more than 8,000 participants), implementation intentions improved goal attainment with an average d = 0.65. Effects were larger with an explicit if-then format, high motivation and a rehearsed plan. — [Konstanz repository](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/eb48c8cf-7855-478e-90ec-1ea6d44f8252/content); [summary](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/glossary/implementation-intentions)
- Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2024), "The when and how of planning: Meta-analysis of the scope and components of implementation intentions in 642 tests," *European Review of Social Psychology*, DOI 10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563. Reported effects across cognitive, affective and behavioral outcomes range from about d = 0.27 to d = 0.66. The paper also builds a taxonomy of plan cues (such as time/place or task juncture) and responses. — [x-mol record](https://x-mol.com/paper/1773961364427554816); [secondary summary](https://goalsandprogress.com/implementation-intentions-research/)
- Bias-corrected estimates from the 2024 paper, reported only by secondary sources: trim-and-fill d = 0.35; robust Bayesian meta-analysis d = 0.15 (95% CI 0.08–0.22). These must be checked against the primary paper. — [secondary blog](https://siliconcanals.com/t-implementation-intentions-when-where-how-goal-achievement/)
- Domain-specific estimates are lower than 2006: healthy eating d = .34; alcohol use g = 0.31. These come from a secondary aggregation. — [secondary blog](https://siliconcanals.com/t-implementation-intentions-when-where-how-goal-achievement/)
- **[unverified this session]** Nickerson & Rogers (2010), *Psychological Science*, Harvard. A get-out-the-vote phone script that asked voters when, where and how they would vote raised turnout by about 4 percentage points among single-voter households. This is a well-known field demonstration of plan-making in a civic ask.
- **[unverified this session]** Milkman, Beshears, Choi, Laibson & Madrian (2011), *PNAS*. Mailers that prompted employees to write down the date and time of their flu shot raised vaccination by about 4 percentage points.

### Inferences
- A talk that leaves people inspired but without a plan is likely to lose roughly half of its effect on intention before behavior changes. The close should therefore turn the feeling into a plan.
- Concrete close technique: (1) name ONE behavior; (2) have each person write or say a plan of the form "When/If [specific time, place or cue], I will [specific first action]"; (3) ask them to picture the main obstacle and add "If [obstacle], then I will [response]." This is Gollwitzer and Oettingen's WOOP / mental contrasting idea, which was not researched this session. (4) Rehearse the plan: say it aloud or tell a neighbor.
- Expect modest real-world effects (d ≈ 0.15–0.35 after bias correction). If-then plans are cheap and beneficial, not magic.

### Gaps
- Could not read the 2024 meta-analysis in full, because the host was blocked. Its exact bias-corrected numbers and the ranking of which cue types work best are unverified.
- Found no study testing implementation intentions formed specifically during a live keynote or speech, as opposed to a lab or questionnaire setting.

## 2. Commitment and consistency, the small first step, and friction (Cialdini, Fogg)

### Takeaway
Commitments do produce behavior change, especially when they are active, public, written and effortful. However, the evidence is mixed and the effects are modest. Foot-in-the-door effects are reliable but small (r ≈ .09–.17). The Fogg Behavior Model (B = MAP) is a widely used practitioner framework: make the action tiny and easy, and give a prompt at the moment motivation is high. It has little direct experimental validation as a model. Evidence grade: B for commitment, B for foot-in-the-door (small), C for Fogg as a validated model (strong face validity, consistent with friction research).

### Cited Findings
- Lokhorst, Werner, Staats, van Dijk & Gale (2013), "Commitment and Behavior Change," *Environment and Behavior* 45(1):3–34 (Leiden University). In a meta-analysis of environmental commitment studies, commitment-making led to behavior change in the short and long term, especially compared with controls. The abstract describes the results as mixed. — [BL catalogue](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100176939518_0x000001); [later citing paper, PMC4624829](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4624829)
- Later work citing Lokhorst et al. says commitment works across environmental behaviors, especially when combined with other strategies. It identifies three mediators, one of them normative: public commitments are kept because breaking them invites social sanctions. — [Springer 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10745-015-9783-8)
- A 2022 systematic review: commitment generally helps pro-environmental behavior, but it appears to be of limited use in the energy domain. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.967318/full)
- Burger (1999), *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 3(4):303–325 (Santa Clara University). A review of foot-in-the-door that reports three earlier meta-analyses, all finding effects above chance but small (overall r = .09–.17). It names self-perception, consistency and commitment as candidate processes and specifies when the effect is most likely to appear. — [Slideshare copy of paper](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/burger/2497673); [CBSM summary](https://cbsm.com/articles/32623-the-foot-in-the-door-compliance-procedure-a-multiple-process-analysis-and-review)
- A secondary source cites foot-in-the-door at r = .17 and door-in-the-face at r = .15, both small even under optimal conditions. — [secondary](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/foot-in-the-door/)
- Fogg Behavior Model (BJ Fogg, Stanford Behavior Design Lab; first paper 2009 at Persuasive '09 as B = MAT, renamed "Prompt" in the 2019 book *Tiny Habits*). A behavior happens when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt converge at the same moment. "Ability" means simplicity: time, money, physical effort, mental effort, social deviance and routine. Recommended order: make sure a prompt exists, then raise ability, and only then try to boost motivation. — [behaviormodel.org (Fogg's official site)](https://behaviormodel.org/); [summary](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/?p=3058)
- **[unverified this session]** Freedman & Fraser (1966), *JPSP*. Homeowners who first agreed to a small request (a small sign or petition) were far more likely to accept a large lawn sign later. This is the classic foot-in-the-door study.
- **[unverified this session]** Cialdini's *Influence* describes commitments as most binding when active, public, effortful and freely chosen.

### Inferences
- Talk-close techniques: get a small, active, public micro-commitment inside the room. Examples: a raised hand, a pledge card filled in and handed over, scanning a QR code to sign up right now, texting a keyword, or telling a neighbor your plan. The ask should be the smallest real first step, not the end goal.
- Remove friction at the moment of peak motivation. Have the sign-up form open, the QR code on screen, pre-filled fields, and time set aside in the session to complete it. Per Fogg, an ask made in the talk but completed "later at home" loses its prompt and its motivation together.
- Because foot-in-the-door effects are small, a single micro-commitment should be combined with plans, norms and follow-up rather than relied on alone.

### Gaps
- Did not find a meta-analytic estimate that directly compares public with private commitment. Lokhorst et al.'s moderator analysis was not visible.
- Found no peer-reviewed validation study of the full Fogg model. Its support is indirect, coming from friction and default-effect research.

## 3. Self-efficacy, hope, fear appeals (EPPM), and the emotions that drive action

### Takeaway
Threat motivates action only when people also believe the recommended action works (response efficacy) and that they can do it (self-efficacy). Fear appeals do work on average (d = 0.29), but efficacy statements strengthen them. For collective action, anger about injustice combined with a sense of group efficacy and a shared identity is the best-supported recipe. Elevation, the feeling of witnessing moral beauty, causally increases helping and volunteering in lab studies. Evidence grade: A for fear appeals plus efficacy, A for SIMCA, B for elevation (small lab studies).

### Cited Findings
- Tannenbaum, Hepler, Zimmerman, Saul, Jacobs, Wilson & Albarracín (2015), "Appealing to Fear," *Psychological Bulletin* (University of Illinois). The analysis covered 127 articles and 248 independent samples. Fear appeals positively affected attitudes, intentions and behaviors, with an average d = 0.29. The hypothesis that fear appeals without efficacy statements are weaker was supported. — [PubMed 26501228](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26501228/); [Table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789790/table/T2)
- Witte & Allen (2000), *Health Education & Behavior*. A meta-analysis widely cited for the claim that fear appeals with efficacy information are more effective. Other meta-analyses found null effects (de Hoog et al., 2007) or negative ones (Peters et al., 2012). — [core.ac.uk citing paper](https://core.ac.uk/download/158301889.pdf)
- Bigsby & Albarracín (2021/2022). Fear appeals influenced behavior more when they included positive *response-efficacy* information ("this works"). Effects did not vary with *self-efficacy* information. — [Annenberg/UPenn PDF](https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2022-06/Bigsby%20and%20Albarracin.pdf)
- Van Zomeren, Postmes & Spears (2008), "Toward an Integrative Social Identity Model of Collective Action (SIMCA)," *Psychological Bulletin* 134(4) (University of Amsterdam / Groningen). Three meta-analyses of 182 effects found that injustice, efficacy and identity each predict collective action with medium effects. Identity bridges the other two. Injustice predicts more strongly when it is emotionally felt (anger), and identity predicts more strongly when politicized. — [VU Amsterdam PDF](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/2391519/Van%20Zomeren%20Psychological%20Bulletin%20134%284%29%202008.pdf); [Climate Advocacy Lab](https://www.climateadvocacylab.org/resource/toward-integrative-social-identity-model-collective-action-quantitative-research-synthesis/)
- Schnall, Roper & Fessler (2010), "Elevation Leads to Altruistic Behavior," *Psychological Science* 21:315–320 (Cambridge / UCLA). Study 1: people who felt elevation were more likely to volunteer for an unpaid follow-up study. Study 2: they spent about twice as long helping an experimenter with a tedious task as amused or neutral participants. Elevation predicted helping, while amusement and happiness did not. Elevation was induced with a video of a musician thanking his mentor. — [PubMed 20424062](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20424062/); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=30644)
- **[unverified this session]** Witte (1992), Extended Parallel Process Model (Michigan State). When perceived threat is high and perceived efficacy is high, people control the danger by acting. When threat is high and efficacy is low, people control their fear instead, through denial, reactance and avoidance.
- **[unverified this session]** Bandura (1977, 1997), self-efficacy theory (Stanford). Its four sources are mastery experiences, vicarious experience (modeling), verbal persuasion and physiological/affective states.
- **[unverified this session]** Haidt (2000/2003) defined "elevation" as the emotion people feel on witnessing moral beauty. It produces a desire to become a better person and to help.

### Inferences
- Structure rule: never raise a threat without, in the same breath, (a) a specific action, (b) evidence that it works, and (c) a demonstration that this audience can do it. Response efficacy ("this works") appears to matter more than generic "you can do it" pep talk.
- Build self-efficacy on stage with Bandura's sources. Show someone like the audience doing it (modeling). Have the audience do the first micro-step during the session (mastery). Break the action into a step that obviously anyone can do.
- Emotion palette: for prosocial asks such as donating or volunteering, an elevating story of a specific person's goodness right before the ask. For civic or collective asks, channel anger at a specific, fixable injustice, together with a "we" identity and evidence that "people like us have won before" (group efficacy). Hope is effectively perceived efficacy combined with a vision of the future.

### Gaps
- Did not retrieve Witte & Allen's (2000) exact effect sizes.
- Elevation studies are small lab experiments. Found no replication in the field or at large scale this session.
- Found no direct research on awe driving action from talks in particular.

## 4. Social proof and norms in the room

### Takeaway
Telling people what most others like them do (a descriptive norm) beats appeals to principle. The closer the reference group ("people in this room"), the stronger the effect. In a live room, visible others acting (hands raised, people walking to the sign-up table) supplies real-time social proof. Evidence grade: B+ (robust field experiments, though the effects depend on context).

### Cited Findings
- Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius (2008), "A Room with a Viewpoint," *Journal of Consumer Research* 35(3):472–482 (Arizona State / Minnesota). Two hotel field experiments; Experiment 1 recorded 1,058 instances of possible towel reuse. A descriptive-norm sign ("most guests reuse") produced significantly more reuse than the standard environmental-protection sign. In Experiment 2, the norm referring to guests who had stayed in *this same room* was the most effective ("provincial norms"), even though the authors considered that group the least meaningful. — [UMN experts](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-room-with-a-viewpoint-using-social-norms-to-motivate-environmen/); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v35y2008i3p472-482.html); [AcaWiki](https://acawiki.org/A_Room_with_a_Viewpoint:_Using_Social_Norms_to_Motivate_Environmental_Conservation_in_Hotels)
- **[unverified this session]** The commonly reported reuse rates are about 35% for the standard message, about 44% for the descriptive norm and about 49% for the same-room norm. The full text could not be read to confirm.
- **[unverified this session]** Cialdini (2003), Petrified Forest study. Signs that described many people stealing (a negative descriptive norm) *increased* theft. Highlighting how common a bad behavior is can backfire.

### Inferences
- In the close, say how many people already do it, especially people like this audience: "Last month, 312 people from this industry signed up." Avoid "too few people do X," which signals that inaction is the norm.
- Make action visible and sequential in the room: "Stand if you've already…", "Raise your hand once you've scanned the code," and show a live counter of sign-ups on screen. This creates local, provincial norms and momentum.
- Planting or prompting early movers creates bandwagon cues, but staged shills cross into deception (see the ethics section).

### Gaps
- Some later replications of hotel-towel norm effects were weaker. These were not retrieved this session.
- Found no controlled study of live audience-wide social proof (for example, on-screen sign-up counters during talks).

## 5. Urgency, scarcity, Monroe's Motivated Sequence, Ganz's "story of now," and Duarte's "new bliss"

### Takeaway
Scarcity cues reliably raise perceived value and purchase intent, according to a meta-analysis of 416 effects. Real deadlines also help defeat procrastination. Fake scarcity is deceptive and increasingly regulated. Monroe's Motivated Sequence (1935) is pedagogically popular, but the one direct experiment found it no more persuasive than reversed or random orderings, only somewhat easier to follow. Ganz's public narrative and Duarte's "what is / what could be" structure are well-developed practitioner frameworks without controlled outcome tests. Both insist on a specific ask. Evidence grade: B for scarcity, C for Monroe (weak or negative evidence), C/D for Ganz and Duarte (practitioner frameworks).

### Cited Findings
- Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal (2022), "Scarcity tactics in marketing: a meta-analysis of product scarcity effects on consumer purchase intentions," *Journal of Retailing* 98(4):741–758 (Bond University / Monash). 131 studies, 416 effect sizes. Scarcity cues raise perceived value and purchase intentions. Demand-based scarcity works best for utilitarian products, supply-based for experiences, and time-based for high-involvement products. — [Monash](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/scarcity-tactics-in-marketing-a-meta-analysis-of-product-scarcity/); [Bond PDF](https://pure.bond.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/182976505/Scarcity_tactics_in_marketing.pdf)
- Micciche, Pryor & Butler (2000), *Psychological Reports* (University of Central Florida). The Monroe-order message, a reversed-order message and a random-order message (n = 21–24 per group) plus a control all produced no difference in attitude change. The Monroe order scored higher on comprehensibility in one of four comparisons. — [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib2000/2702)
- A public-speaking textbook notes that almost no research shows Monroe's sequence to be more persuasive than other structures. — [Saylor textbook](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_stand-up-speak-out-the-practice-and-ethics-of-public-speaking/s20-03-organizing-persuasive-speeches.html); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe%27s_motivated_sequence)
- Marshall Ganz (Harvard Kennedy School), "What Is Public Narrative: Self, Us & Now" (2009 working paper). The story of self explains why I am called; the story of us gives shared values and experience; the story of now gives the urgent challenge, the choice and hope. Each story follows a challenge, choice, outcome arc. Training guides adapting Ganz say the story of now must end with a specific, actionable ask (vote, volunteer, donate, attend). — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-c817-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content); [Ganz PDF](https://changemakerspodcast.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Ganz-WhatIsPublicNarrative08.pdf); [Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.org/the-power-of-story-the-story-of-self-us-and-now/)
- Nancy Duarte, *Resonate* (2010). Great talks oscillate between "what is" and "what could be." The call to action is the last turn of that pattern, but the talk should end on the "new bliss," a vivid picture of the world after the idea is adopted, rather than on the ask itself. — [Duarte blog](https://duarte.com/?p=13247); [Duarte talk page](https://kinstastg.duarte.com/resources/talks/the-secret-structure-of-great-talks/); [Talks at Google transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/talksatgoogle/illuminate-nancy-duarte-patti-sanchez-talks-at-google)

### Inferences
- Synthesis of close order (practitioner consensus plus research): (1) the "now," a concrete and urgent challenge; (2) the specific ask, one action, made tiny; (3) efficacy proof that it works and that you can do it; (4) social proof; (5) an in-room micro-commitment plus an if-then plan; (6) end on the vivid new-bliss picture. This is Monroe's "visualization" step and Duarte's new bliss.
- Urgency should be real and explained: a real deadline, a real capacity limit, a real event date. Real deadlines help because the intention-behavior gap widens with delay. Fabricated countdowns and "only 3 seats left" claims are deceptive.
- Monroe's sequence is useful for clarity, not as a proven persuasion engine. Its value comes from containing the active ingredients (need plus a specific solution, visualization and an explicit action step), not from the order itself.

### Gaps
- Found no controlled studies testing Ganz's public narrative or Duarte's structure on behavioral outcomes.
- Found no research isolating the effect of deadlines in live-event calls to action.

## 6. Sales from the stage and seminar closing techniques: effectiveness and ethical/legal critiques

### Takeaway
There is essentially no peer-reviewed research on the conversion rates of "selling from the stage." The evidence base is regulatory enforcement. The FTC has repeatedly sanctioned seminar and "wealth training" operations whose stage pitches and upsells relied on deceptive earnings claims and pressure tactics. Evidence grade: D for effectiveness claims, B for regulatory and legal risk.

### Cited Findings
- FTC v. Response Marketing Group (May 2023). The company and TV real-estate personalities Dean Graziosi and Scott Yancey settled over the marketing of "wealth creation" training programs sold through free events and upsells. The amount is reported inconsistently: $16.7 million total in one source, versus "$1.7 million from the celebrity endorsers" in a law-firm summary, which was the FTC's first monetary settlement with celebrity endorsers. The two figures may refer to different components. Consumers became eligible for refunds. — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/85000); [Hunton law-firm summary](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/keeping-it-real-estate-ftc-secures-first-monetary-settlement-against-celebrity-endorsers)
- FTC (1995), Robbins Research International / Tony Robbins. The FTC alleged Franchise Rule violations in selling seminar franchises for $5,000–$90,000. The defendants paid $221,260 in redress. This case concerned franchise disclosure, not pitch tactics. — [FTC 1995](https://www.ftc.gov/opa/1995/05/robbins.htm)
- The FTC has refunded money to customers of the deceptively marketed "Amazing Wealth System" seminar/training scheme ($9.1M+). — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/impact-data-security-regulations-ecommerce?page=81); [FTC refunds report 2023](https://ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/ftc-refunds-to-consumers-2023.pdf)
- **[unverified this session]** The FTC Cooling-Off Rule (16 CFR 429) gives buyers 3 days to cancel sales of $25 or more made at temporary locations such as hotel or convention rooms. This is directly relevant to back-of-room sales. The FTC Business Opportunity Rule and its 2024–2025 earnings-claims enforcement also apply.

### Inferences
- Common stage-selling devices (a "today-only" price, a "first 10 people" bonus, a "run to the back" rush, a price stack and a drop) borrow from scarcity, commitment and social proof. Regulators treat them as deceptive when the scarcity is fake, the earnings claims are unsubstantiated, or the refund terms are hidden.
- An ethical version: a genuine offer, transparent price, honest results data with typical outcomes, a genuine deadline with its reason stated, and an easy refund. Audiences who are emotionally aroused are more vulnerable, so the speaker carries a higher duty of care.

### Gaps
- Found no peer-reviewed data on stage-sale conversion rates. Industry figures such as "10–20% of the room" circulate in marketing blogs without verifiable methods and should not be cited as fact.
- The Response Marketing Group settlement amounts conflict between sources; the FTC press release should be checked.

## 7. Follow-up after the talk: reminders, accountability, monitoring

### Takeaway
What happens after the talk matters as much as the close. Timely, personalized reminders that point to an action already "reserved" for the person increase behavior by a few percentage points at scale. Monitoring progress improves goal attainment (d = 0.40), and the effect is larger when progress is recorded and reported publicly. Evidence grade: A.

### Cited Findings
- Milkman et al. (2021), *PNAS*, megastudy (Penn Wharton Behavior Change for Good). N = 47,306 patients, 19 text-message nudges before a primary-care visit. Texts raised flu vaccination by an average of 5%. The best message reminded patients twice and said the vaccine was "reserved for you." — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nas:journl:v:118:y:2021:p:e2101165118); [Arizona repository](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/659876)
- Milkman et al. (2022), *PNAS*. 689,693 Walmart pharmacy patients received 22 text reminders. The average effect was +2.0 percentage points (6.8% over control). The best messages again framed the shot as waiting for or reserved for the person and were sent on multiple days. — [BU repository](https://open.bu.edu/items/9ca99b93-9b4e-4a97-ae53-7b5753366ed8); [comminit](https://global.comminit.com/content/680000-person-megastudy-nudges-encourage-vaccination-pharmacies)
- Harkin, Webb, Chang, Prestwich, Conner, Kellar, Benn & Sheeran (2016), *Psychological Bulletin* 142(2):198–229. 138 randomized studies, N = 19,951. Prompting progress monitoring improved goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI 0.32–0.48). The effects were larger when progress was reported to others or made public and when it was physically recorded. Most studies were in health. — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/87431); [Science for Work summary](https://scienceforwork.com/blog/goal-monitoring/)

### Inferences
- Collect contact details (SMS or email) inside the talk as the micro-commitment itself. Send a reminder within 24–72 hours that restates the person's own if-then plan. Send a second reminder near the action date ("your spot is reserved"). Repeated, ownership-framed reminders work best.
- Build accountability: buddy pairs formed in the room, a public progress board or community, and check-ins on a schedule. Make monitoring easy (a tracker or a weekly prompt) and, with consent, visible to others.
- Plan for the effects to decay. Multiple touches spaced over time beat one post-event email.

### Gaps
- Found no research measuring how quickly post-talk motivation decays, for example over days.
- Spaced-retrieval and learning-transfer literature was not researched this session.

## Ethics boundary (cross-cutting)
- **Legitimate influence:** truthful information, genuine deadlines and limits, real social-proof numbers, voluntary and reversible commitments, actions that serve the audience's own goals, and easy opt-out or refund.
- **Manipulation or deception, which also carries legal risk:** fabricated scarcity or countdowns, planted "buyers" or shills, unsubstantiated earnings or results claims, pressure that exploits peak emotional arousal to extract large payments on the spot, hidden refund terms, and fear appeals without any real efficacy path. These conflict with FTC deception standards and with the Cooling-Off Rule for temporary-venue sales (the latter unverified this session). See section 6 sources.
- **Heuristic:** would the audience feel the technique was fair if it were fully disclosed to them afterward? (A "transparency test," common in persuasion ethics; no specific source retrieved this session.)
