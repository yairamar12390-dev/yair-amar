# Persuasion at the Micro-Level of Speech: How Specific Words and Sentences Change Influence

Research notes, current as of November 2026. URLs are kept here; the final product cites by researcher name only.

**Evidence grading used throughout:**
- **A**: robust, meta-analytic support.
- **B**: several consistent studies, with moderators.
- **C**: a single or contested landmark study.
- **D**: failed or largely failed replications.

**Note on verification:** Items marked [DK] come from established domain knowledge. Their DOI link was not opened in this session, so the report writer should treat the exact figures for [DK] items as needing a spot-check. Every other item was confirmed against the search or fetch results in this session.

**Context: how big are word effects at all?** O'Keefe & Hoeken (2021, *Frontiers in Psychology*) reviewed 30 message-design variations across 1,149 studies. The median mean effect was about r = .10. Most single word or phrasing choices make small differences. They matter at scale (a large audience or repeated exposure) and when several are stacked together. This expectation should frame every tool below — [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.664160/text)

## 1. Source credibility, message sidedness, language intensity, and powerful vs powerless speech

### Takeaway
Who seems to be speaking is shaped by the words they choose, and it matters as much as the arguments.
- Refutational two-sided messages (raise the counterargument, then answer it) beat one-sided ones. Merely mentioning the other side without refuting it backfires (grade A).
- Powerless speech markers, especially hesitations, cut credibility and persuasion (grade B; moderated by expertise and audience).
- Intense language helps high-credibility speakers and hurts low-credibility ones (grade B).

### Cited Findings
**Hovland's Yale Communication Research Program (1940s–50s)** [DK]
- Hovland & Weiss (1951, *Public Opinion Quarterly*, Yale) attributed identical messages to high- or low-credibility sources. High credibility produced more immediate opinion change. After about 4 weeks the difference shrank: low-credibility messages gained and high-credibility ones lost. This is the "sleeper effect" — [DOI 10.1086/266350](https://doi.org/10.1086/266350)
- Kumkale & Albarracín (2004, *Psychological Bulletin*, meta-analysis) found the sleeper effect is reliable only under specific conditions [DK]:
  - the discounting cue (e.g., "this source is unreliable") comes *after* the message;
  - the arguments are strong;
  - the audience is motivated or able to process.
  
  Grade: B — [DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.130.1.143](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.130.1.143)

**One-sided vs two-sided messages**
- O'Keefe (1999, *Communication Yearbook* 22; Univ. of Illinois / Northwestern) meta-analysed 107 studies with about 20,000 participants:
  - Refutational two-sided messages were more persuasive and more credible than one-sided messages on non-advertising topics.
  - Non-refutational two-sided messages were *less* persuasive than one-sided ones.
  - The audience's initial position and education had little moderating effect.
  - In advertising, sidedness made little difference.
  
  Grade: A — [synapsesocial summary](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2503528511); [spring.org.uk summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=12521)
- Allen (1991, *Western Journal of Speech Communication*), a separate meta-analysis, reached the same conclusion: refutational two-sided > one-sided > non-refutational two-sided — [informahealthcare abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10570319109374395)
- The exact r for the refutational advantage (often quoted as about r = .08) could not be confirmed this session. See Gaps.

**Powerful vs powerless speech**
- Erickson, Lind, Johnson & O'Barr (1978, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*; Duke Law & Language Project) studied simulated courtroom testimony [DK]:
  - Powerless style includes hedges ("sort of", "I guess"), hesitations ("uh", "um"), tag questions ("…, isn't it?"), intensifiers and polite forms.
  - Witnesses using the powerless style were rated less credible and less convincing than witnesses using the powerful style.
  
  — [DOI 10.1016/0022-1031(78)90015-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031(78)90015-X)
- A secondary source reports, citing the 1978 work, that powerful-style witnesses were rated more trustworthy, convincing and competent. It also reports that hedges and tag questions lowered effectiveness, and that hesitations were the *most* damaging — [Revista de Psicología del Trabajo 2017](https://journals.copmadrid.org/jwop/art/j.rpto.2017.03.001)
- Burrell & Koper (1998, meta-analysis, *Communication Quarterly*) found that powerless language tends to reduce persuasion and perceived credibility. Grade: B — [Revista de Psicología del Trabajo 2017, citing Burrell & Koper](https://journals.copmadrid.org/jwop/art/j.rpto.2017.03.001)
- Lawrence Hosman (University of Southern Mississippi) and colleagues dissected the individual markers:
  - Speaker expertise interacts with hesitations in shaping attitude change.
  - Hosman, Huebner & Siltanen (2002, *Journal of Language and Social Psychology*) note that contradictory results make overall conclusions difficult, and they add need for cognition as a moderator.
  
  — [Hosman, Huebner & Siltanen 2002 PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%206/HosmanHuebnerSiltanen2002.pdf)
- Hedges are not uniformly harmful:
  - Some hedges (high-likelihood hedges such as "very likely", and personal-perspective hedges such as "I think") can *increase* persuasion, because they signal a confident, honest communicator (Wharton working paper).
  - O'Barr (1982) argued that hedges reduce credibility.
  - Meyer (1997) argued that hedges can strengthen a claim.
  
  — [Wharton working paper "Hedges"](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/Hedges-1.63.docx)

**Language intensity** [DK]
- Bowers (1963) and Burgoon's Language Expectancy Theory (Burgoon, Jones & Stewart, 1975; Burgoon & Miller, 1985):
  - Intense or emotional words help a source who is *expected* to use them, typically a credible, high-status speaker.
  - They violate expectations and backfire for low-credibility sources, and for highly involved or hostile audiences.
  
  Hamilton & Hunter's (1998) meta-analysis broadly supports a credibility × intensity interaction. Grade: B — [Google Scholar: Hamilton Hunter 1998 language intensity meta-analysis](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Hamilton+Hunter+1998+language+intensity+attitude+change+meta-analysis)

### Inferences
**Speaker tools:**
1. **"Raise and refute."** Say: "Some of you are thinking X. Here is why X doesn't hold…" Never raise an objection you don't answer.
2. **Strip hesitations first.** "Um", "uh" and fillers are the most damaging powerless marker. Replace them with a silent pause.
3. **Remove tag questions and apologetic hedges** ("sort of", "kind of", "I just think"). Keep calibrated hedges that signal honesty ("very likely", "the data strongly suggest").
4. **Earn intensity before using it.** Build credibility in the opening with low-intensity, precise language. Escalate intensity only once the audience grants you status.
5. **Sleeper effect.** If you expect your credibility to be attacked after you speak, put your strongest arguments first and keep them memorable. Their impact may grow over weeks even if the audience discounts you.

### Gaps
- The exact effect sizes for O'Keefe (1999) refutational vs one-sided messages (commonly quoted r ≈ .08) and for Burrell & Koper (1998) were not verified from the full texts.
- No recent (post-2015) pre-registered, large-scale replication of the powerless-speech effect was located.

## 2. Fluency and truth: repetition, rhyme, processing ease, and concreteness

### Takeaway
Statements that are easier to process feel truer: repeated, rhyming, concrete. The repetition-induced "illusory truth effect" is among the most robust findings here (grade A). The biggest gain comes from the second exposure. Linguistic concreteness raises perceived truth (B) and, in field data, customer satisfaction and spending (B). Rhyme-as-reason is a classic single study with limited replication (C).

### Cited Findings
**Illusory truth effect**
- Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino (1977, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*; Temple/Villanova) asked people to rate plausible trivia statements across sessions two weeks apart. Repeated statements were rated increasingly true, whether they were true or false [DK] — [DOI 10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80012-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80012-1)
- Dechêne, Stahl, Hansen & Wänke (2010, *Personality and Social Psychology Review*; Univ. of Cologne / Basel) meta-analysed about 51 studies conducted before 2008 (some sources say 70):
  - within-items effect: d = .39, 95% CI [0.30, 0.49];
  - between-items effect: d = .50, 95% CI [0.43, 0.57].
  
  — [Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 2021 critique](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-021-01995-w)
- **Caveat:** A 2021 *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* paper argues that the 2010 estimate may be inflated by publication bias: no funnel plot was reported, and the coded data were unavailable. Its authors concluded that a valid meta-analysis of the whole literature is not possible. Even so, the effect itself is not in doubt; it has been widely replicated, including with known-false statements (Fazio et al., 2015) — [same source](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-021-01995-w)
- Hassan & Barber (2021, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*) found that the largest increase in perceived truth occurs at the *second* exposure, with diminishing gains after that — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-021-00301-5)

**Rhyme-as-reason**
- McGlone & Tofighbakhsh (2000, *Psychological Science*; Lafayette College) presented unfamiliar aphorisms in rhyming form ("Woes unite foes") or a non-rhyming equivalent ("Woes unite enemies"). The rhyming forms were judged more accurate, and participants denied that rhyme influenced them. The effect disappeared when participants were warned to separate a statement's poetic quality from its content [DK]. Grade: C, because there are few direct replications — [DOI 10.1111/1467-9280.00282](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00282)

**Concreteness and truth**
- Hansen & Wänke (2010, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*; Univ. of Basel) found that the same fact phrased in concrete language (descriptive action verbs, specific terms) was judged more probably true than abstract phrasings. They explain this through construal level and imaginability [DK]. Grade: B — [Google Scholar: Hansen Wänke 2010 "Truth from language and truth from fit"](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Hansen+W%C3%A4nke+2010+Truth+from+language+and+truth+from+fit)
- Packard & Berger (2021, *Journal of Consumer Research* 47(5):787–806; York University / Wharton) ran five studies, including text analysis of over 1,000 real employee–customer interactions (phone calls at a U.S. firm; email service at an online retailer):
  - Concrete language ("I'll go get those *navy Nike sneakers*" vs "I'll go get those") increased satisfaction, willingness to buy, and actual purchases.
  - In the field data, +1 SD of concreteness was associated with about +9% satisfaction and at least +13% spending.
  - The mechanism: concrete language signals that the listener is *being heard*.
  
  Grade: B — [Schulich news](https://schulich.yorku.ca/news/the-secret-to-superior-customer-service-be-concrete/); [Knowledge@Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/how-language-boosts-customer-satisfaction/)

### Inferences
**Speaker tools:**
1. **Repeat the core claim at least twice, phrased identically**, for example at the open and the close. The second repetition delivers most of the gain.
2. **Coin a rhyming or alliterative tagline** for your key message (the "If it doesn't fit, you must acquit" pattern). Use it ethically: it boosts perceived truth without adding evidence.
3. **Replace abstract nouns with concrete, picturable words, numbers, names and verbs.** Not "we improved outcomes", but "37 more kids in Be'er Sheva read at grade level by June."
4. **Echo the audience's exact words back concretely.** In Q&A, name the specific thing the questioner raised to signal listening (Packard & Berger).
5. **Ethics flag:** The same mechanisms make repeated falsehoods feel true. Warn audiences about opponents' slogans to inoculate them.

### Gaps
- No large pre-registered replication of McGlone & Tofighbakhsh's rhyme-as-reason effect was located this session.
- No Hebrew-language test of rhyme-as-reason or of the illusory truth effect was found.

## 3. Reasons, freedom, labels, "we", and generic "you"

### Takeaway
- Giving *a* reason ("because…") increases compliance for small requests. The original effect is famous but has only partial and mixed replication (C).
- Reminding people they are free to refuse ("but you are free to…") increases compliance. However, the effect is much weaker in low-risk-of-bias studies (B−, possibly inflated).
- Noun labels ("be a voter") showed a large effect in one paper and failed in several field replications (D).
- Leaders' "we/us" language robustly predicts electoral success (B; correlational).
- Generic "you" helps people draw meaning from negative experiences (B).

### Cited Findings
**"Because": the Xerox / copier study**
- Langer, Blank & Chanowitz (1978, *JPSP* 36:635–642; Harvard / CUNY Graduate Center) had a confederate ask to cut in line at a library copier [DK]:

  | Request | No reason | Placebic reason ("…because I have to make copies") | Real reason ("…because I'm in a rush") |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Small (5 pages) | ~60% complied | ~93% | ~94% |
  | Large (20 pages) | ~24% | ~24% | ~42% |

  The authors called the empty reason "placebic". The finding is that mindless compliance occurs for low-stakes requests only.
  
  — [DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.36.6.635](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.36.6.635)
- **Replication status** [DK]: Folkes (1985, *JPSP*) found that compliance depended on the *content and controllability* of the reason, not on the word "because" itself, which contradicts the strong "magic word" reading. The cell sizes in the original were small (about 15 per cell). Grade: C. The popular claim that "because" works by itself is overstated — [Google Scholar: Folkes 1985 mindlessness or mindfulness Langer replication](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Folkes+1985+Mindlessness+or+mindfulness+a+partial+replication+and+extension+of+Langer+Blank+and+Chanowitz)

**"But you are free" (BYAF)**
- Guéguen & Pascual (2000) ran the original French field study ("…but you are free to accept or refuse") — [HAL record](https://hal-amu.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-02976670)
- Carpenter (2013, *Communication Studies*; Western Illinois Univ.) meta-analysed 42 studies (about 22,000 participants, per the brief) and found a reliable increase in compliance. The exact effect statistic was not verified this session.
- Fillon, Souchet, Pascual & Girandola (2023, *Meta-Psychology*; Univ. Bordeaux / Aix-Marseille) ran a pre-registered update:
  - 52 studies, N = 28,759, 74 effect sizes: g = 0.44, 95% CI [0.36, 0.51].
  - In the 7 low-risk-of-bias studies only, g = 0.11, 95% CI [−0.18, 0.40], which is non-significant.
  - Most recent studies are underpowered.
  
  Grade: B− (the effect may be real but smaller than believed) — [Meta-Psychology](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2640)

**Labeling ("be a voter" vs "vote")**
- Bryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck (2011, *PNAS*; Stanford / Harvard) found that noun wording ("How important is it to you to *be a voter*?") raised registration interest and actual turnout (validated records) compared with verb wording ("…to *vote*") — [FORRT Replication Atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1073/pnas.1103343108/)
- Gerber, Huber, Biggers & Hendry (2016, *PNAS*; Yale) ran a large field experiment in primaries and found no effect. A 2018 *Political Psychology* replication and a pre-registered 2016-presidential-election study (*Behavioural Public Policy*, 2020) also found no effect ("Voting behavior is unaffected by subtle linguistic cues").
- Bryan, Walton & Dweck replied that low-salience primaries were not an authentic replication context.
- The FORRT atlas lists these replications as failed. Grade: D — [Cambridge BPP](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/abs/voting-behavior-is-unaffected-by-subtle-linguistic-cues-evidence-from-a-psychologically-authentic-replication/7CA9BE69C3213D5030A2BD11A5C53450); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087036); [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/media/file-research-paper/field-experiment-shows-subtle-linguistic-cues-might-not-affect-voter)
- Related: Bryan, Adams & Monin (2013) reported "don't be a cheater" > "don't cheat". It is subject to the same caution [DK].

**Leaders' "we" language**
- Steffens & Haslam (2013, *PLoS ONE* 8(10):e77952; Univ. of Queensland) analysed campaign launch speeches from all 43 Australian federal elections (1901–2013):
  - Winners used more "we/us" than losers in 80% of elections.
  - Winners used "we/us" about once every 79 words; losers about once every 136 words.
  - Odds ratio for winning: 2.24 per +1 SD of we-references.
  
  The design is correlational. It is consistent with the social identity theory of leadership (Haslam, Reicher & Platow, *The New Psychology of Leadership*). Grade: B — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3806781); [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/26621)

**Generic "you"**
- Orvell, Kross & Gelman (2017, *Science* 355:1299–1302; Univ. of Michigan) found that generic "you" ("*you* win some, *you* lose some") is used spontaneously when people make meaning from negative experiences. Using it creates psychological distance and makes the lesson feel universal [DK]. Grade: B — [DOI 10.1126/science.aaj2014](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaj2014)

### Inferences
**Speaker tools:**
1. **Always attach a genuine reason to an ask.** "Because" plus a real, relevant reason works. Don't rely on placebic reasons for anything consequential: with large requests, only real reasons helped.
2. **Close a call-to-action with an explicit freedom reminder:** "…but of course, the choice is entirely yours." It is cheap and probably helps, but expect a modest effect.
3. **Identity labels ("be a voter", "be a donor") are optional garnish, not a proven lever.** Don't claim them as science-backed.
4. **Shift from "I" and "you" to "we" and "us"** when describing goals, values and achievements. Count your "we" rate and aim for roughly 1 per 80 words in a mobilising speech.
5. **Use generic "you" to universalise lessons from personal hardship:** "When you lose someone, you learn…" It turns a personal anecdote into a shared truth.

### Gaps
- No modern, high-powered, pre-registered direct replication of Langer et al. (1978) was confirmed this session.
- Carpenter's (2013) exact pooled effect size was not verified.
- Orvell et al. tested meaning-making, not persuasion directly. Its persuasive use is an extrapolation.

## 4. Speech rate, certainty language, and expressed confidence

### Takeaway
- Moderately fast speech is generally associated with higher perceived competence and persuasion (B; moderated by whether the message is pro- or counter-attitudinal).
- Expressed certainty usually helps.
- Karmarkar & Tormala (2010) show an expectancy-violation twist: experts gain by voicing some uncertainty, and novices gain by voicing certainty, but only when the arguments are strong (B).

### Cited Findings
- Miller, Maruyama, Beaber & Valone (1976, *JPSP* 34:615–624; USC) found that faster speech (about 190 wpm) was rated more credible and knowledgeable, and was more persuasive, than slow speech (about 100 wpm) [DK] — [DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.34.4.615](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.34.4.615)
- Smith & Shaffer (1991, 1995, *PSPB*) refined this [DK]:
  - Fast speech helps for counter-attitudinal messages because it reduces counter-arguing.
  - It can hurt for pro-attitudinal messages, where slower speech lets agreeable thoughts form.
  
  — [Google Scholar: Smith Shaffer speech rate persuasion](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Smith+Shaffer+1991+speed+of+speech+persuasion+counterattitudinal)
- Karmarkar & Tormala (2010, *Journal of Consumer Research* 36(6):1033–1049; Stanford GSB / Harvard), "Believe Me, I Have No Idea What I'm Talking About", used restaurant reviews:
  - Low-expertise sources stimulated more involvement and were more persuasive when they expressed certainty.
  - High-expertise sources were more persuasive when they expressed some uncertainty.
  - The mismatch increases message processing, so it helps only when the arguments are strong.
  
  — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/believe-me-i-have-no-idea-what-im-talking-about?cmpid=main); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:v:36:y:2010:i:6:p:1033-1049)
- Tormala and colleagues note that the classic certainty advantage comes from domains with an objectively correct answer, such as witnesses or stock advisers — [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/confidence-may-not-be-convincing-when-recommending-products-or-services/)

### Inferences
**Speaker tools:**
1. **Default to a brisk pace**, especially with skeptical audiences. With friendly audiences you can slow down for key lines.
2. **If you are the recognised expert**, drop one calibrated note of doubt before your strongest argument: "I'll be honest, I'm not 100% sure, but here's what the evidence shows…" It invites listeners to lean in.
3. **If you lack formal authority, speak with conviction.** Avoid hedges.
4. Either way, **the arguments must be strong.** The certainty twist amplifies processing, and weak arguments then get punished.

### Gaps
- No post-2015 meta-analysis of speech rate and persuasion was found.
- The Karmarkar & Tormala effect sizes were not extracted, and no large pre-registered replication was located.

## 5. Questions vs assertions, gain/loss framing, emotional words and virality (awe, elevation)

### Takeaway
- Rhetorical questions increase *scrutiny*. They help strong arguments and hurt weak ones when the audience is uninvolved (B).
- Gain vs loss framing produces near-zero average differences in persuasion (A: the "framing myth" is debunked at the aggregate).
- High-arousal emotion words, including awe, drive sharing (B).

### Cited Findings
**Rhetorical questions** [DK]
- Petty, Cacioppo & Heesacker (1981, *JPSP* 40:432–440; Ohio State / Missouri) tested messages that summarised arguments as rhetorical questions ("Wouldn't you agree that…?"):
  - Under low personal involvement, rhetorical questions *increased* processing: strong arguments became more persuasive and weak ones less.
  - Under high involvement, they *disrupted* processing.
  
  — [DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.40.3.432](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.40.3.432)

**Gain vs loss framing**
- O'Keefe & Jensen (2007, *Journal of Health Communication*), disease prevention: 93 studies, N = 21,656. Gain frames had a tiny advantage, r = .03, attributable mainly to dental-hygiene messages — [PubMed 17934941](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17934941/)
- O'Keefe & Jensen (2009, *Journal of Communication*), disease detection: loss frames had a negligible advantage, r = −.04 — [CBSM summary](https://www.cbsm.com/articles/33455-the-relative-persuasiveness-of-gain-framed-and-loss-framed-messages-for-encouraging-disease-detectio)
- O'Keefe & Jensen (2006, *Communication Yearbook*), all topics: 165 effect sizes, N = 50,780. Loss-framed appeals are not generally more persuasive. Grade: A — [Benjamins / summary](https://benjamins.com/online/ebop/publications/49796)
- **Attribute framing is different from goal framing** [DK]:
  - Tversky & Kahneman (1981, *Science* 211:453–458; Hebrew University / Stanford): the "Asian disease" problem. "200 people *will be saved*" led most to choose the sure option; "400 people *will die*" led most to choose the gamble — [DOI 10.1126/science.7455683](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683)
  - Levin & Gaeth (1988): beef labelled "75% lean" was rated better than "25% fat". Attribute and risky-choice framing are robust (Levin, Schneider & Gaeth, 1998). Gain/loss *goal* framing in persuasion is not.

**Emotional words and virality** [DK]
- Berger & Milkman (2012, *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2):192–205; Wharton) analysed about 7,000 *New York Times* articles for appearance on the most-emailed list:
  - Positive content was more viral than negative.
  - High-arousal emotions predicted sharing: awe (+1 SD → about +30% odds), anger (about +34%), anxiety (about +21%).
  - Low-arousal sadness reduced sharing (about −16%).
  
  Grade: B (large field correlational study plus experiments) — [DOI 10.1509/jmr.10.0353](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353)
- On elevation: Haidt's moral-elevation work (Algoe & Haidt, 2009) shows that witnessing virtue elicits warmth and a desire to emulate or help. Its direct persuasion effects at the word level are less established. Grade: C — [Google Scholar: Algoe Haidt 2009 elevation](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Algoe+Haidt+2009+witnessing+excellence+elevation+gratitude+admiration)

### Inferences
**Speaker tools:**
1. **Use rhetorical questions only to wrap up strong arguments**, for example "So — is this a risk we can afford?" Never use them to cover weak points; they spotlight weakness.
2. **Don't agonise over "save" vs "lose" in a call to action.** The average difference is negligible. Do attend to attribute labels: "90% success rate" beats "10% failure rate".
3. **For shareable lines, use high-arousal words.** Awe ("staggering", "breathtaking scale"), justified anger, urgency. Avoid wistful, sad framings if you want spread.
4. **Elevation stories** (a concrete act of moral beauty) build warmth and help-intentions. Pair them with a specific ask.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of rhetorical-question effects was located.
- Berger & Milkman's figures should be spot-checked against the paper.
- No study directly isolating "awe words" in live speeches was found.

## 6. Hebrew-language and Israeli studies on word-level persuasion

### Takeaway
Israeli scholarship contributes landmark word-framing work and recent conflict-context language studies:
- Tversky & Kahneman's framing (Hebrew University roots).
- Liberman's "game label" study.
- Construal-level theory (Trope & Liberman), which underpins concreteness effects.
- The native-language advantage for peace proposals: Hebrew > English (Grant, Keysar & Maoz).
- Noun labelling to reduce anger over concessions (Reifen Tagar, Hameiri & Halperin).

Few studies test pure Hebrew word choice. This is a genuine gap.

### Cited Findings
- **Tversky & Kahneman (1981)** (both trained at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem): risky-choice framing, "saved" vs "die" [DK] — [DOI 10.1126/science.7455683](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683)
- **Liberman, Samuels & Ross (2004, *PSPB*; Varda Liberman, then IDC Herzliya / Reichman, with Stanford)** [DK]:
  - The identical Prisoner's Dilemma was labelled either "Wall Street Game" or "Community Game".
  - About two-thirds cooperated under the Community label, versus about one-third under Wall Street.
  - The label predicted cooperation better than peers' predictions of individuals' reputations did.
  
  Grade: B (later conceptual replications exist, with smaller effects) — [DOI 10.1177/0146167204264004](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167204264004)
- **Trope & Liberman (2010, *Psychological Review*; NYU / Tel Aviv University)**: construal-level theory. Abstract vs concrete language maps onto psychological distance. It is the theoretical basis for Hansen & Wänke's concreteness–truth findings [DK] — [DOI 10.1037/a0018963](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018963)
- **Grant, Keysar & Maoz (2022, *Journal of Conflict Resolution*; Univ. of Chicago / Hebrew University), "Lingua Franca as a Hidden Barrier to Conflict Resolution"**:
  - Israeli-Jewish participants rated a trust-building and security proposal more favourable to their side when it was presented in Hebrew than in English.
  - The English version produced more negative emotion; after reading the Hebrew version, participants reported less hatred.
  - The effect was replicated in a second sample.
  
  — [UChicago news](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/conflict-resolution-more-successful-using-native-language-research-shows); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2022-10-conflict-resolution-successful-native-language.pdf)
- **Grant, Shahwan, Maoz & Keysar (2024, *PLOS ONE*)**: the same proposal delivered in Hebrew by a Palestinian speaker with a heavier accent was rated worse for Israel than when delivered with no detectable accent — [UChicago knowledge repository PDF](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/14031/files/journal.pone.0311373.pdf)
- **Reifen Tagar, Hameiri & Halperin (Reichman / Tel Aviv / IDC)**, presented at APS 2026 and reported as forthcoming or in *Psychological Science*:
  - Statements about concessions phrased with noun labels (vs verbs) led to significantly less anger toward the Israeli government and toward Palestinians.
  - Publication status should be verified.
  
  — [APS 2026 listing](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/conventions/archive/2026annual/paper/60592/); [APS "What is Grammar's Role in Conflict?"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=144525)
- **Reaction-time study with 85 Jewish Israeli students**: responses were slower to left-wing than to right-wing political words, suggesting automatic negative valence attached to left-wing political terms in Hebrew. This is relevant to word choice when addressing Israeli audiences — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/study-people-show-verbal-hesitation-towards-left-wing-political-terms/)
- **Foreign-language effect in Israel**: a pre-registered study (N = 398) found that deciding in a foreign language eliminated home bias in investment choices, while deciding in native Hebrew did not. This is consistent with foreign language attenuating framing and loss-aversion biases — [Judgment & Decision Making / Cambridge PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/EB184633BF5457119BA303604EB5480B/S1930297525100181a.pdf/foreign-language-mitigates-home-bias.pdf)
- **Hebrew-language LLM vs human persuasion**: a pre-registered study (N = 200, Hebrew University co-authors, arXiv 2025) compared LLM and human persuaders on Israeli civil-policy questions, conducted entirely in Hebrew — [arXiv 2503.01844](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01844v3)

### Inferences
**Speaker tools for Israeli audiences:**
1. **Deliver emotionally sensitive or conciliatory proposals in Hebrew**, not English, even to bilingual audiences.
2. **Name the "game".** The label you give a situation ("a community effort" vs "a competition") shapes cooperative behaviour more than many arguments do.
3. **Frame painful concessions as nouns and states** ("a compromise", "an agreement") rather than actions done to the group ("to give up", "to concede"). This may reduce anger. The evidence is preliminary.
4. **Be aware that political-camp words carry automatic valence in Hebrew.** Prefer neutral or shared-identity terms (e.g., "we Israelis") over camp-coded labels.

### Gaps
- No Israeli replication of the illusory truth effect, rhyme-as-reason, "because", BYAF or powerless-speech effects in Hebrew was found.
- Hebrew-specific features remain untested for persuasion: grammatical gender in second-person address, the formal–informal register gap, and the effect of a binyan or root echo in slogans.
- The full methods of the Reifen Tagar et al. noun study and its final publication status are unverified.
- The 2025 *Journal of Applied Social Psychology* paper "Mind the cue: subtle linguistic cues influence support for anti-refugee policies" could not be opened. Its sample country is unconfirmed, and it may be Israeli.
