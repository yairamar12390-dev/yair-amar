# Influencing Small Audiences (1-on-1, boardrooms, workshops, 5–30 people): The Science and a Toolkit

Scope note: Research done in November 2026 with web search. Two full-text hosts (centaur.reading.ac.uk, ssoar.info) were blocked in this session, so some numbers come from abstracts and repository pages. Evidence grades: STRONG = meta-analyses or many replications; MODERATE = several solid studies, some moderators or disputes; WEAK = single or old studies, small samples, mostly field anecdotes; FAILED/MIXED = replications failed or came back mixed.

---

## 1. Listening as influence (Itzchakov & Kluger; motivational interviewing)

### Takeaway
High-quality listening (attentive, non-judgmental, empathic) is one of the best-supported small-setting influence tools. It does not "push" a view. It lowers the speaker's defensiveness, so they hold their own attitudes in a more complex, less extreme, more open way. Motivational interviewing (MI) applies the same principle clinically: across 100+ trials it has a small, durable effect against weak comparisons but no advantage over other active treatments. GRADE: listening → attitude openness = MODERATE-TO-STRONG (many lab experiments plus a large work meta-analysis); MI = STRONG for a small effect.

### Cited Findings
- Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro (2017, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*), "I am aware of my inconsistencies but can tolerate them": four lab experiments manipulated listening quality across many attitude topics. Good listening reduced speakers' social anxiety, which reduced defensive processing. Speakers then reported more attitude ambivalence (they saw both sides) and could tolerate inconsistencies. Keywords include attitude extremity and defensive processing. Affiliations: University of Haifa / Hebrew University. — [HUJI CRIS](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/i-am-aware-of-my-inconsistencies-but-can-tolerate-them-the-effect/); [Haifa CRIS](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/i-am-aware-of-my-inconsistencies-but-can-tolerate-them-the-effect/)
- Itzchakov, Weinstein, Leary, Saluk & Amar (2024, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*), "Listening to understand: the role of high-quality listening on speakers' attitude depolarization during disagreements": four experiments compared poor, moderate and high-quality listening, using a video vignette (Study 1) and live interactions (Studies 2–4). High-quality listening produced perceived and actual attitude depolarization. The pathway ran through "positivity resonance" and nondefensive self-reflection. The effects held across attitude certainty and attitude morality (moralized attitudes). Weinstein is at the University of Reading. — [Reading repository record](https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/114502); [manuscript PDF (blocked in session)](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/114502/1/Listening%20in%20disagreements%20manuscript%20in%20press.pdf); [PERC-hosted copy](https://perc.wa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Listening_Depolirization.pdf)
- Itzchakov and colleagues: perceiving others as responsive lessens prejudice, mediated by intellectual humility and attitude ambivalence. — [Itzchakov lab page](https://guyitzchakov.haifa.ac.il/publications/perceiving-others-as-responsive-lessens-prejudice-the-mediating-roles-of-intellectual-humility-and-attitude-ambivalence)
- An Itzchakov registered report found that empathic listening satisfies speakers' psychological needs and well-being. It did not directly deepen later solitude experiences. This is an example of the lab publishing a partial null. — [Itzchakov lab page](https://guyitzchakov.haifa.ac.il/publications/empathic-listening-satisfies-speakers-psychological-needs-and-well-being-but-doesnt-directly-deepen-solitude-experiences-a-registered-report)
- Kluger & Itzchakov (2022), "The Power of Listening at Work," *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* 9:121–146. This is a narrative review spanning marketing, nursing, law, social work, psychology and education. It proposes "episodic listening theory": listening can create a fleeting state of togetherness in which the pair think creatively together. — [search summary / RealKM](https://realkm.com/2022/12/27/the-power-of-listening-at-work-part-1-consequences-facilitators-and-hindrances/); [ma.x-mol record](https://ma.x-mol.com/paper/1485470692349853696)
- Kluger et al. (2023), "A Meta-analytic Systematic Review and Theory of the Effects of Perceived Listening on Work Outcomes": a registered review with three-level meta-analyses of 664 effect sizes and 400,020 observations. Perceived listening correlated with job performance at r = .21 (95% CI .13–.29; ρ = .23). — [HUJI CRIS](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-systematic-review-and-theory-of-the-effects-of-pe/); [Haifa CRIS](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-systematic-review-and-theory-of-the-effects-of-pe/)
- Lundahl, Kunz, Brownell, Tollefson & Burke (2010, *Research on Social Work Practice* 20(2):137–160), a meta-analysis of motivational interviewing over 119 studies:
  - Against weak comparison groups, MI had a small, statistically significant, durable effect (g = 0.28).
  - Against specific active treatments, the effect was not significant (g = 0.09).
  - Moderators were feedback (MET), delivery time, manualization, group vs individual delivery, and ethnicity.
  - Sources: [Utah State repository](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/sswa_facpubs/199); [PDF](https://media.wcwpds.wisc.edu/website-docs/wcwpds/resources/motivational-interviewing/Research-on-Social-Work-Practice.pdf)
- Magill et al. (2018), a meta-analysis of the MI process ("technical, relational and conditional process models"): it tests whether MI-consistent therapist behavior elicits client "change talk," which then predicts outcomes. — [PubMed 29265832](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29265832/)

### Inferences
- In a 1-on-1 meeting or a tense board discussion, listening is a precondition for persuasion. It loosens the other person's grip on their current position, and your argument can then land. The 2024 depolarization effects held even for moralized attitudes. That matters for contentious meetings.
- MI shows that "influence through questions" works about as well as other active approaches, not better. It reliably beats doing nothing or simply giving advice. The honest claim is "as good as other active approaches, and far less resistance-provoking."
- Practical listening behaviors drawn from the Itzchakov/Kluger paradigm (background knowledge of their manipulations, consistent with the abstracts above): keep full attention, with no phone and no interrupting. Ask open questions. Reflect and paraphrase. Do not judge or rush to give advice. Show non-verbal attentiveness.
- Possible conflict of interest: the 2024 JPSP paper has a co-author surnamed Amar. The report writer should check whether this connects to the end user, so it is cited neutrally.

### Gaps
- I could not read effect sizes and Ns for the 2017 and 2024 Itzchakov papers in full text, because the host was blocked.
- I did not retrieve newer MI meta-analyses (2015–2026) to update Lundahl, such as Frost et al. 2018 or the review of reviews.

## 2. Rapport and mimicry (chameleon effect; mimicry in negotiation)

### Takeaway
Chartrand & Bargh (1999) is a landmark, but its replication record is mixed. Automatic imitation itself is robust. The claim that "being mimicked makes people like and trust you" is fragile and depends on context, and it can backfire if detected. Maddux, Mullen & Galinsky (2008) found that subtle strategic mimicry improved negotiation deals, but there is little direct replication. GRADE: mimicry → liking = MIXED/WEAK-MODERATE; strategic mimicry in negotiation = MODERATE-WEAK (two experiments, mediated by trust, few replications).

### Cited Findings
- Chartrand & Bargh (1999, *JPSP* 76(6):893), "The chameleon effect": the FORRT replication atlas lists one replication, Förster et al. (2022). It found a mimicry effect for smiling but not for foot-shaking or face-rubbing. Outcome: MIXED. — [FORRT replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1037/0022-3514.76.6.893/)
- A preregistered replication of Chartrand & Bargh Study 1 began data collection in November 2022 (University of Münster symposium). I found no results. — [Münster abstract](https://indico.uni-muenster.de/event/3176/contributions/5328/contribution.pdf)
- Hale & Hamilton (2016, *Scientific Reports*, UCL):
  - In virtual reality, rapport was higher toward a mimicking avatar in one study.
  - A preregistered follow-up found NO effect of mimicry on rapport ratings, trust ratings or implicit trust behavior.
  - The authors concluded that being mimicked does not always increase rapport or trust.
  - Sources: [UCL PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1531017/1/srep35295.pdf); [summary record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4Lv8vxV9)
- Hale & Hamilton (2016, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*): an in-depth review suggests that "the link from mimicry to liking and other positive outcomes may be fragile." — [Hamilton PDF](https://www.antoniahamilton.com/haleHamilton_NBR_2016.pdf)
- Cracco, Genschow et al. (2018, *Psychological Bulletin*), a meta-analysis: automatic imitation is a robust, largely automatic process. This concerns imitation itself, not its social payoff. — cited in [Farmer et al., Acta Psychologica 2021 (UCL PDF)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10118680/1/1-s2.0-S0001691820305503-main.pdf)
- A secondary blog claims a meta-analytic rapport effect of about d ≈ 0.3, with moderators (for example, getting caught mimicking) that can flip it negative. NOT VERIFIED; it is a marketing blog and I could not find the primary meta-analysis. — [Yu-kai Chou blog](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/chameleon-effect-chartrand-bargh-mimicry-rapport/)
- Research on mimicry duration: whether mimicry helps or costs depends on its timing. — [PsychOpen short report](https://spb.psychopen.eu/index.php/spb/article/download/13261/13261.html?inline=1)
- Penn State study on same-race vs cross-race mimicry: it "was unable to conclusively replicate and extend previous findings." — [Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-affiliative-consequences-of-same-race-and-cross-race-mimicry/)
- Maddux, Mullen & Galinsky (2008, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 44:461–468), "Chameleons bake bigger pies and take bigger pieces":
  - Study 1: negotiators instructed to subtly mimic did better individually, and their dyads created more joint value. Mimickers claimed most of the extra value, but not at the opponent's expense.
  - Study 2: mimicry increased the likelihood of reaching a deal in a negotiation with no obvious solution. Interpersonal trust mediated the effect.
  - The often-quoted "67% vs 12.5% deal rate" figure could NOT be verified in this session.
  - Sources: [Kellogg page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2008/chameleons-bake-bigger-pies-and-take-bigger-pieces-strategic); [Maddux UNC PDF](https://willmaddux.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/15846/2018/04/JESP-Mimicking-and-Negotiations.pdf); [AMA summary](https://www.amanet.org/articles/mimicking-increases-odds-of-success-in-negotiation-says-study/)

### Inferences
- Do not teach mimicry as a technique to "install rapport." If it is detected it can backfire, and the evidence is fragile. Safer advice: match tempo, energy and vocabulary, and mirror the other person's key words when paraphrasing. Paraphrasing overlaps with high-quality listening, which is better supported.
- In negotiations, the trust-mediated benefit suggests that whatever signals "we are alike and in sync" helps. The specific body-mirroring method is not well replicated.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the exact deal-rate percentages in Maddux et al. (2008) because the full text was blocked.
- I did not locate a peer-reviewed meta-analysis of mimicry's effect on liking and prosocial outcomes, nor results of the Münster preregistered replication.

## 3. Questions vs telling (self-persuasion, question-behavior effect, Socratic questions, advice-seeking, follow-up questions)

### Takeaway
Asking well works better than telling in small settings, through several channels:
- People are more persuaded by reasons they generate themselves (self-persuasion).
- Being asked to predict or state an intention nudges behavior slightly (question-behavior effect, d ≈ 0.14–0.24).
- Asking for advice raises perceived competence.
- Follow-up questions increase liking.

GRADE: question-behavior effect = STRONG for a small effect (two meta-analyses); advice-seeking = MODERATE (one multi-study paper, solid moderators); follow-up questions → liking = MODERATE (disputed but defended); self-persuasion = MODERATE (classic, not re-verified here).

### Cited Findings
- Wood, Conner, Miles et al. (2016, *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 20(3):245–268), a meta-analysis of 116 published tests:
  - Intention or self-prediction questions had a small positive effect on later behavior (d+ = 0.24).
  - The effect was larger for desirable, low-risk and easy behaviors, and in student samples.
  - The results gave little support to dissonance, attitude-accessibility or fluency explanations.
  - Publication bias was judged modest; trim-and-fill still left a smaller but significant effect.
  - By domain (reported in a commentary): health d = 0.29, prosocial d = 0.19, risky behaviors null.
  - Sources: [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/88297/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4931712); [Frontiers commentary](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00879/full)
- Wilding, Conner et al. (2016, *European Review of Social Psychology*): k = 66 studies (94 tests), d = 0.14. Effects were smaller in better-controlled studies. — [White Rose PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/108516/1/Wilding_ERSP.pdf)
- Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer (2015, *Management Science* 61(6):1421–1435), "Smart People Ask for (My) Advice":
  - People who seek advice are perceived as MORE competent, contrary to their fear of looking incompetent.
  - The effect is moderated by task difficulty (stronger when the task is hard), advisor egocentrism and advisor expertise (it holds when the request is in the advisor's domain).
  - Proposed mechanism: asking flatters the advisor's ego.
  - Affiliations: Harvard Business School / Wharton.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:61:y:2015:i:6:p:1421-1435); [MIT Sloan Review](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/?p=35844)
- Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017, *JPSP* 113(3):430–452), "It Doesn't Hurt to Ask: Question-Asking Increases Liking":
  - In two get-to-know-you experiments plus speed-dating data, people who asked more questions, especially follow-up questions, were better liked.
  - In speed dating, more follow-ups meant more second-date agreements.
  - People did not predict this effect.
  - Mechanism: perceived responsiveness.
  - Sources: [PubMed/APS summary](https://psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/asking-questions-increases-likability.html); [Harvard Kennedy School](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cpl/publications/it-doesnt-hurt-ask-question-asking-increases-liking)
- Dispute: a 2019 *JPSP* critique by Hebrew University authors ("Question Asking as a Dyadic Behavior") argued that the conclusions weaken once the dyadic data structure is modeled. The original authors replied in 2019 ("It helps to ask," JPSP 117(6)) that the results are robust. — [HUJI CRIS](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/question-asking-as-a-dyadic-behavior/); [PubMed 31714109](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31714109/)

### Inferences
- Self-persuasion (Elliot Aronson; for example, the 1990s hypocrisy-induction / condom studies, where people advocated safe sex to others and then confronted their own lapses): this is background knowledge, NOT re-verified with sources in this session. Treat it as a classic principle: people believe arguments they produce more than arguments they hear. In a workshop: "What would be the three strongest reasons for X?" rather than presenting the three reasons yourself.
- Socratic questioning: I found no persuasion-specific meta-analysis in this session. It overlaps conceptually with MI's evoking questions and with self-persuasion.
- Board tactic: ask decision-makers to state an intention ("Would you be willing to pilot this in Q1?"). The question-behavior effect is small but real, and stronger for easy, desirable actions.
- Ask senior stakeholders for advice in their own domain on a genuinely hard question. Advice-seeking raises competence perceptions and gets them invested (the mechanism is advisor ego).

### Gaps
- I did not search primary sources for Aronson's self-persuasion work (for example, Aronson 1999, *American Psychologist*, "The power of self-persuasion"), or for Socratic questioning effects on attitudes.
- I found no study on question-asking specifically in pitch or boardroom settings.

## 4. Small-group dynamics (conformity, minority influence, polarization, first offers / anchoring, sequencing)

### Takeaway
Asch-type conformity still replicates at about a third of responses (Franzen & Mader 2023: 33%), even with incentives (25%), and extends to political opinions (38%). Minorities rarely win public agreement. They do shift private and indirect attitudes (Wood et al. 1994, 97 studies), especially when consistent. First offers anchor outcomes strongly (Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001), with boundaries: strong alternatives, knowing the other side's reservation price, a firm own target, and complex negotiations. GRADE: Asch conformity = STRONG (replicated 2023); minority influence (indirect/private) = MODERATE-STRONG (meta-analysis); first-offer anchoring = STRONG (many replications; complexity moderates).

### Cited Findings
- Franzen & Mader (2023, *PLOS ONE*, University of Bern), "The power of social influence: A replication and extension of the Asch experiment":
  - Design: N = 210; 5 confederates and 1 naive participant per group.
  - Error rate on the standard line task was 33%, replicating Asch. With monetary incentives it fell to 25%.
  - Conformity on political statements was 38%.
  - Openness was the only Big Five trait linked to conformity.
  - Limitations: student sample, small incentives.
  - The authors expected Asch to be overstated and found it wasn't.
  - Sources: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10686423/); [Bern PDF](https://boris.unibe.ch/189635/1/journal.pone.0294325.pdf); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/2023/12/skeptical-scientists-revisit-solomon-aschs-classic-conformity-experiments-and-are-stunned-by-the-results-214872)
- Wood, Lundgren, Ouellette, Busceme & Blackstone (1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 115(3):323–345), a meta-analysis of 97 minority-influence experiments:
  - Majorities beat minorities on direct public agreement and on direct private agreement.
  - On INDIRECT private measures, minorities and majorities did not differ.
  - Minority impact was most marked on measures private from the source and only indirectly related to the appeal.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_influence); [abstract record](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/997659633); [Wood et al. 1996 PDF (USC)](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/Wood.Pool_.Leck_.Purvis.1996_Self-definition_defensive_processing_and_influence.pdf)
- Consistency is often cited as the key to minority influence (Moscovici's blue-green slides, 1969). This is supported mostly by secondary or teaching sources found here. — [Save My Exams (secondary)](https://www.savemyexams.com/a-level/psychology/aqa/25/revision-notes/social-influence/minority-influence/minority-influence/)
- Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001, *JPSP* 81:657–669), "First offers as anchors" (Northwestern / Universität Würzburg at the time):
  - Across three experiments, whichever party made the first offer, buyer or seller, got the better outcome. First offers strongly predicted final settlements.
  - The effect held face-to-face and over email.
  - It disappeared when the receiver focused on their own alternatives (Exp 1), the opponent's reservation price (Exp 2) or their own target (Exp 3).
  - More extreme first offers led to better outcomes for the offerer.
  - Sources: [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2001/first-offers-as-anchors-the-role-of-perspective-taking); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/archive/when-to-make-the-first-offer-in-negotiations); [PON Harvard](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/making-the-first-move/)
- Later meta-analysis plus experiments (Leuphana University): negotiation complexity moderates both the benefits and the costs of making the first offer. The first-mover advantage is not guaranteed in complex multi-issue deals. — [Leuphana PDF](https://pubdata.leuphana.de/bitstreams/118ae0f8-2859-4b3b-8e3e-1a26e6fb9eee/download)

### Inferences
- Speaking-order tactic for decision meetings: the first concrete number or proposal on the table anchors discussion, just as first offers do. Put your framing or proposal first, and make it concrete.
- To defuse others' anchors and conformity cascades, collect independent written estimates or votes before discussion. This is the Asch logic: one dissenter breaks conformity. That last point is background knowledge from Asch's 1950s variations (conformity dropped sharply with a single ally) and was NOT re-verified here.
- If you are the minority voice in a boardroom, expect little visible agreement in the room. Consistency and principled argument may still shift private, later opinions. Follow up 1-on-1 after the meeting.
- Group polarization: discussion among like-minded members tends to push the group toward a more extreme version of its initial leaning (Isenberg 1986 meta-analysis; Myers & Lamm). This is background knowledge, NOT verified in this session.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve sources on group polarization (Isenberg 1986, Sunstein), hidden-profile research (Stasser & Titus 1985), or who-speaks-first effects (for example, HiPPO, or Bonner's research on expertise in groups).
- I did not verify the Asch one-ally variation or Moscovici's original consistency numbers from primary sources.

## 5. Warmth and trust signals at close range (eye contact, names, touch, self-disclosure / 36 questions, humor)

### Takeaway
Close-range signals cut both ways. Direct eye contact INCREASES resistance in listeners who already disagree (Chen et al. 2013). Reciprocal, escalating self-disclosure reliably builds closeness (Aron 1997; Collins & Miller 1994 meta-analysis). The "Midas touch" tipping effect rests on old field studies, has no dedicated meta-analysis found here, and is culturally and ethically fraught in professional settings. GRADE: eye contact backfiring with skeptics = MODERATE (two studies, one paper); self-disclosure → liking = STRONG (meta-analysis); 36 questions / fast friends = MODERATE-STRONG (replicated, for connection rather than commitment); touch = WEAK (old field studies, culturally sensitive); names and humor = not established in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Chen, Minson, Schöne & Heinrichs (2013, *Psychological Science* 24(11):2254–2261), "In the Eye of the Beholder: Eye Contact Increases Resistance to Persuasion" (Universität Freiburg / Harvard Kennedy School):
  - Eye-tracking: more time spent looking at a speaker's eyes predicted LESS attitude change, mainly when listeners already disagreed.
  - Study 2: instructed eye-gazing produced less persuasion than mouth-gazing.
  - Proposed mechanism: direct gaze signals dominance or threat.
  - Sources: [HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/eye-beholder-eye-contact-increases-resistance-persuasion); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/10/131002092629.htm); [Freiburg](https://www.psychologie.uni-freiburg.de/abteilungen/psychobio/team/publikationen/chenheinrichs2013)
- Collins & Miller (1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(3):457–475), a meta-analysis that separated three effects:
  - (a) People who make intimate disclosures are liked more than low disclosers.
  - (b) People disclose more to those they already like.
  - (c) People come to like those they have disclosed to.
  - Moderators were paradigm, disclosure type and discloser gender.
  - Sources: [PubMed 7809308](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/); [UCSB PDF](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/collins/nancy/UCSB_Close_Relationships_Lab/Publications_files/Collins%20and%20Miller,%201994.pdf)
- Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone & Bator (1997, *PSPB* 23(4):363–377), "The Experimental Generation of Interpersonal Closeness" (Stony Brook):
  - The "fast friends" procedure of 36 escalating self-disclosure questions produced more closeness than 36 small-talk questions.
  - Later work (Sprecher 2021, Leckfor et al. 2025, text-based) replicates greater connectedness, liking and perceived responsiveness.
  - The authors cautioned that the procedure does not create loyalty, dependence or commitment.
  - Sources: [Greater Good Science Center](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness); [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A4287769/view); [ARU news](https://www.aru.ac.uk/news/can-36-questions-really-change-your-love-life)
- Crusco & Wetzel (1984, *PSPB*), "The Midas touch":
  - Brief server touch raised tips above a no-touch control, with no difference between touch types or by customer gender.
  - Another restaurant study found no touch effects on ratings of the server or the experience; touch may work without awareness.
  - Turkish field replications of US tipping tactics diverged from US results, which points to cultural sensitivity (it is unclear whether touch was among the tactics).
  - I found no touch-specific meta-analysis.
  - Sources: [Psychology Today reference list](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/let-their-words-do-the-talking/201207/six-tips-to-get-higher-tips); [Synapse Social](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2052874641); [Emerald – Turkey replications](https://www.emerald.com/ejm/article/60/02/544/1337133/Tipping-to-the-top-Three-conceptual-replications)

### Inferences
- Eye contact: with a skeptical decision-maker, soften gaze. Look at shared material and break gaze naturally rather than "locking eyes," which signals dominance. With an audience that already agrees, eye contact is not shown to hurt.
- Self-disclosure in a workshop or sales relationship: use appropriate, reciprocal, gradually escalating disclosure, such as a short story of your own mistake or doubt. Effect (c) means getting THEM to disclose also raises their liking for you, which loops back to listening and questions.
- Touch: the evidence is thin, dated and from service contexts. In professional, cross-cultural and post-#MeToo settings the ethical and cultural risks outweigh any small gain. Recommend a handshake or culturally normative greeting only.
- Names and humor: I found no sources in this session. Background knowledge suggests evidence that hearing one's own name captures attention (the cocktail-party effect), and that affiliative humor supports leader liking, which humor meta-analyses also report. Self-deprecating humor works mainly for high-status speakers. All of this is UNVERIFIED here.

### Gaps
- I found no sourced evidence on name use in persuasion (for example, Howard, Gengler & Jain 1995 on name remembering and compliance) or on humor in small groups and negotiation (for example, Bitterly, Brooks & Schweitzer 2017 on humor and status; the Mesmer-Magnus 2012 humor-at-work meta-analysis). The report writer should either omit these or mark them as unverified.
- I found no modern, preregistered replication of the touch effect.

## 6. Pitching and boardroom persuasion (passion vs preparedness; storytelling; structure vs big stage)

### Takeaway
In investor-style pitches, perceived PREPAREDNESS (substance, thought-through plan), not displayed passion, predicted VC funding decisions (Chen, Yao & Kotha 2009). Later work debates how passion is conceptualized. For small expert audiences, depth and responsiveness to questions likely matter more than stagecraft. GRADE: preparedness > passion = MODERATE (lab experiment plus field study; later studies partly contest the framing).

### Cited Findings
- Chen, Yao & Kotha (2009, *Academy of Management Journal* 52(1):199–214), "Entrepreneur Passion and Preparedness in Business Plan Presentations: A Persuasion Analysis of Venture Capitalists' Funding Decisions":
  - Xiao-Ping Chen et al., University of Washington.
  - They treated VC decisions as persuasion (the unimodel) and built measures of perceived passion and preparedness inductively.
  - In a lab experiment and a field study, preparedness, not passion, affected funding decisions.
  - Sources: [Peeref](https://peeref.com/works/65267612); [SUFE newsletter](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361611)
- Critique: Chen et al. treated enthusiasm, preparedness and commitment as independent constructs. Cardon, Mitteness & Sudek (2016/2017, *Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice*) argue that perceived passion includes displayed commitment, which does influence angel investors. — [Cardon et al. ETP PDF](https://effectuation.org/hubfs/Journal%20Articles/2017/05/Cardon_et_al-2016-Entrepreneurship_Theory_and_Practice-1.pdf?hsLang=en); [Pace accepted manuscript](https://activityinsight.pace.edu/mcardon/intellcont/CardonMittenessSudek_ETP_accepted_2016-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- Boardroom and pitch structure differs from a big stage: the audience interrupts, asks probing questions and controls the agenda. Preparedness signals (a clear plan, anticipated objections, fluent answers to tough questions) are what get evaluated. Build an appendix of backup slides and rehearse the Q&A more than the monologue.
- Show commitment (what you have already invested or risked) rather than performed enthusiasm. This reconciles Chen et al. with Cardon et al.
- Combine with section 4: lead with a concrete anchor (the ask, a valuation, a target) and with section 3: end on an intention question.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve sources on storytelling in sales or narrative transportation (for example, the van Laer et al. 2014 meta-analysis on narrative transportation), on thin-slice pitch judgments (for example, Huang & Pearce 2015 on angel "gut feel"), or on gender bias in pitch evaluation (for example, Brooks et al. 2014 PNAS; Kanze et al. 2018). The report writer should treat these as unverified if used.
- I found no direct study comparing small-room vs large-stage pitch structure.

## 7. Graded practical toolkit (derived from sections 1–6)

### Takeaway
The best-supported small-audience tools work by lowering defensiveness and engaging the other person's own reasoning: listening, questions, advice-seeking, reciprocal disclosure. Structural tools (anchoring first, independent input before discussion) shape group outcomes reliably. Body-language "hacks" (mimicry, eye contact, touch) are weak or can backfire.

### Cited Findings
- STRONG: Listen at high quality before arguing. Reflect, paraphrase, don't interrupt, no advice-rushing. Supported by Itzchakov/Kluger experiments and the 664-effect-size meta-analysis (r = .21 with performance). — [HUJI CRIS 2017](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/i-am-aware-of-my-inconsistencies-but-can-tolerate-them-the-effect/); [HUJI CRIS meta](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-systematic-review-and-theory-of-the-effects-of-pe/)
- STRONG (small effect): Ask intention or self-prediction questions to close: "Will you…?" (d ≈ 0.14–0.24). — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4931712)
- STRONG: Make your concrete proposal or number first, unless the other side has a strong alternative or full information, or the deal is highly complex. — [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2001/first-offers-as-anchors-the-role-of-perspective-taking); [Leuphana](https://pubdata.leuphana.de/bitstreams/118ae0f8-2859-4b3b-8e3e-1a26e6fb9eee/download)
- STRONG: Conformity is real (about 33%). To get honest input, gather private written opinions before open discussion. To build momentum, let early supporters speak first. — [PMC Franzen & Mader](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10686423/)
- STRONG: Use reciprocal, gradually escalating self-disclosure and invite theirs. — [PubMed Collins & Miller](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/)
- STRONG for a small effect (MI): use evoking questions so the other person voices their own reasons for change ("change talk"). — [USU Lundahl](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/sswa_facpubs/199)
- MODERATE: Ask for advice from senior or expert stakeholders on hard problems in their domain. — [RePEc Brooks et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:61:y:2015:i:6:p:1421-1435)
- MODERATE: Ask follow-up questions to signal responsiveness and raise liking. — [APS summary](https://psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/asking-questions-increases-likability.html)
- MODERATE: In pitches, prioritize demonstrable preparedness and commitment over performed passion. — [Peeref Chen et al.](https://peeref.com/works/65267612)
- MODERATE: As a minority voice, stay consistent and expect delayed, private shifts. Follow up 1-on-1. — [abstract record Wood et al.](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/997659633)
- MODERATE (caution): With a skeptical listener, avoid sustained direct eye contact. Use shared visuals and a softer gaze. — [HKS Chen et al.](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/eye-beholder-eye-contact-increases-resistance-persuasion)
- WEAK/MIXED: Mimicry. At most, use natural verbal mirroring through paraphrase. Never imitate overtly. — [FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1037/0022-3514.76.6.893/); [Hale & Hamilton NBR](https://www.antoniahamilton.com/haleHamilton_NBR_2016.pdf)
- WEAK + ethical risk: Touch. Do not recommend it beyond culturally normal greetings. — [Emerald Turkey replications](https://www.emerald.com/ejm/article/60/02/544/1337133/Tipping-to-the-top-Three-conceptual-replications)

### Inferences
- Suggested meeting sequence (synthesized, not tested as a package):
  1. Open with listening or questions.
  2. Ask for their advice.
  3. Share a brief, honest disclosure.
  4. Put a concrete anchor or proposal on the table.
  5. Handle objections by reflecting before answering.
  6. Close with an intention question.
  7. For groups, collect private input first.
- The Israeli contribution is central. Itzchakov (University of Haifa) and Kluger (Hebrew University) supply the strongest "influence through listening" line of evidence. The 2019 dyadic critique of follow-up-question research also came from Hebrew University.

### Gaps
- No study tests these tools in combination, or specifically in 5–30 person workshop settings. Most evidence is dyadic or lab-based.
- The humor, names, group polarization, storytelling and self-persuasion primary sources still need verification (see gaps in sections 3–6).
