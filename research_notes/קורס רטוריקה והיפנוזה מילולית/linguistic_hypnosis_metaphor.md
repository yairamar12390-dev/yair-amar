# Linguistic (Ericksonian) Hypnosis, Hypnotic Language Patterns and Metaphor: Ethical Use by Public Speakers

Research notes, compiled 2026-11-16. Evidence grades used throughout: **STRONG** (meta-analyses or repeated controlled replications), **MODERATE** (several controlled studies with some caveats or mixed replications), **WEAK** (few or small studies, mostly correlational or clinical reports), **NO EVIDENCE** (claims rest on anecdote, training lore or marketing).

Method note: these notes come from web searches plus abstracts and secondary summaries. Full-text fetches of cambridge.org and pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov were blocked in this session (those hosts are not on the session's allowed network list), so several effect sizes are reported from secondary sources and flagged as such. Hebrew examples are constructed by the researcher for teaching purposes and are labelled as illustrations, not findings.

## 1. Milton H. Erickson's approach: indirect suggestion, permissive language, utilization, confusion, interspersal, therapeutic stories

### Takeaway
Erickson (1901–1980) built a clinical style around indirect, permissive suggestion, using whatever the client brings ("utilization"), and teaching stories and anecdotes. His key texts are clinical case reports and transcripts, not controlled tests. Erickson himself was also often highly directive, so "Ericksonian = indirect" is a simplification.

### Cited Findings
- The core text on indirect suggestion is *Hypnotic Realities: The Induction of Clinical Hypnosis and Forms of Indirect Suggestion* (Erickson, E. L. Rossi & S. I. Rossi, 1976, Irvington), with a foreword by André Weitzenhoffer. — [Reference listing, informahealthcare](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/14779757.2003.9688312)
- The interspersal technique comes from Erickson's paper "The interspersal hypnotic technique for symptom correction and pain control," *American Journal of Clinical Hypnosis*, 8(3), 198–209 (1966). It is reprinted in *The Collected Papers of Milton H. Erickson*, Vol. 4 (ed. E. L. Rossi), pp. 262–278. — [Reference listing, informahealthcare](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/14779757.2003.9688312)
- Jeffrey K. Zeig, founder of the Milton H. Erickson Foundation in Phoenix, teaches indirect methods such as "the interspersal technique, the confusion technique and the use of anecdotes" in his professional workshops. — [Jeffrey Zeig, Workshops for Professionals](https://jeffreyzeig.com/workshops-for-professionals/); Foundation resources: [Erickson Foundation](https://erickson-foundation.org/resources)
- Bandler & Grinder's *Patterns of the Hypnotic Techniques of Milton H. Erickson, M.D.* (Vol. 1, 1975) is the source of the "Milton Model". — [archive.org PDF](https://archive.org/download/andrew.chumbleyazoetia/Richard%20Bandler%20And%20John%20Grinder%20-%20Patterns%20Of%20The%20Hypnotic%20Techniques%20Of%20Milton%20Erickson.pdf)
- Barber's (1985) definitions, as summarised in the research literature: indirect suggestions are permissive and offer choices ("you may find..."), while direct suggestions are authoritative and prescribe a specific response ("your arm is getting heavy"). — [Scite summary of direct/indirect literature](https://scite.ai/authors/victor-neufeld-aKYp)
- Practitioner commentary notes that Erickson "used highly directive suggestions with some patients". The idea that his work was purely indirect is a later NLP-era simplification. — [Direct vs Indirect Suggestion (practitioner article)](https://hypnotherapy-training.squarespace.com/articles/2016/6/20/direct-vs-indirect-suggestion)
- A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs of Ericksonian hypnotherapy (2015–2025), published in *Psychiatry International*, says it is the first comprehensive review focused on the Ericksonian approach. Its authors find "initial empirical support" across physical and psychological conditions but say the evidence is limited by small samples and heterogeneity. Pooled effect sizes were not retrieved. — [Çınaroğlu et al., MDPI Psychiatry International 7(1):16](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-5318/7/1/16); [repository record](https://acikerisim.beykoz.edu.tr/handle/123456789/658)

**Technique definitions with speaker adaptations.** The definitions follow standard Ericksonian literature. The examples, including the Hebrew ones, were written by the researcher.

| Technique | What it is | Public-speaking example (EN / HE) | Evidence grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indirect / permissive suggestion | Suggesting a response as possible or optional rather than commanding it | "You might notice, as I speak, which of these ideas already feels familiar." / "אולי תשימו לב, תוך כדי, איזה מהרעיונות כבר מרגיש לכם מוכר." | WEAK as a superior method (see Section 3). It is a fine stylistic choice for rapport. |
| Utilization | Folding whatever is happening (noise, doubt, the audience's mood) into the message | A phone rings: "And just like that phone, some ideas interrupt us exactly when we need them." / "בדיוק כמו הטלפון הזה, יש רעיונות שמפריעים לנו בדיוק כשהם נחוצים." | NO EVIDENCE from controlled tests. It is widely praised by improvisation and rhetoric teachers. |
| Confusion technique | Overloading conscious processing (paradox, complex syntax) so a simple clear suggestion that follows is grasped with relief | Rarely appropriate on stage. A mild version is a puzzling question followed by a crisp answer. | NO EVIDENCE outside case reports. It carries an ethical risk (Section 6). |
| Interspersal | Embedding key words or suggestions within an ongoing, seemingly neutral narrative | Weaving "calm", "confident" and "ready" into a story about a sailor | WEAK. The source is a 1966 case-based paper. |
| Therapeutic anecdote / metaphor | A story whose structure mirrors the listener's problem and models a resolution | "My grandfather planted an olive tree he knew he'd never harvest..." (a long-term vision speech) | MODERATE through narrative-transportation research (Section 4), not through Erickson-specific trials |

### Inferences
- For a speaking course, Erickson is best taught as a model of audience-centred, permissive, story-rich communication. His case-report claims about bypassing the conscious mind should not be presented as established fact.
- "Utilization" overlaps heavily with recognised improvisation skills (acknowledging and reframing the room). Students can practise it safely without any hypnotic framing.

### Gaps
- Pooled effect sizes and risk-of-bias ratings from the 2026 Ericksonian meta-analysis could not be retrieved.
- Jay Haley's *Uncommon Therapy* (1973) and Stephen Gilligan's *Therapeutic Trances* (1987) are standard texts. No publisher or primary page was fetched for them in this session.
- No controlled study was found that isolates the confusion or interspersal technique outside clinical case series.

## 2. The "Milton Model" (Bandler & Grinder, 1975): language patterns, definitions and speaking examples

### Takeaway
The Milton Model is a catalogue of vague, presupposing and indirect language patterns that Bandler and Grinder drew from transcripts of Erickson's sessions. Many of the patterns are real linguistic phenomena (presupposition, questions, rhythm, repetition), and some of them have independent research support. The claim that they work as covert commands bypassing awareness has **no controlled evidence**.

### Cited Findings
- The Milton Model originates in Bandler & Grinder's 1975 *Patterns of the Hypnotic Techniques of Milton H. Erickson*. — [archive.org PDF](https://archive.org/download/andrew.chumbleyazoetia/Richard%20Bandler%20And%20John%20Grinder%20-%20Patterns%20Of%20The%20Hypnotic%20Techniques%20Of%20Milton%20Erickson.pdf)
- NLP training lexicons claim that hidden or embedded commands "can bypass conscious resistance". They cite no studies. — [Landsiedel NLP lexicon](https://www.landsiedel.com/en/nlp/nlp-lexikon/versteckte-befehle.php)
- Marketing sources claim embedded commands raise results by "10–20%" without data. — [beehiiv marketing post](https://inbxr.beehiiv.com/p/this-gets-people-to-click-without-realizing-why)
- A targeted search found no peer-reviewed controlled experiment testing embedded commands or the Milton Model as persuasion devices. Results were practitioner, sales and podcast material. — e.g. [academia.edu "Embedded commands" (non-peer-reviewed)](https://www.academia.edu/33686587/Embedded_commands); [Plaintiff trial lawyer tips](https://plaintifftriallawyertips.com/?p=1828)

**Pattern glossary.** Definitions follow Bandler & Grinder (1975). The examples were written by the researcher. Hebrew notes are linguistic observations by the researcher.

| Pattern | Definition | English speaking example | Hebrew example / note | Evidence grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Presupposition | Building an assumption into the sentence so that it is taken for granted rather than argued | "When you use this tomorrow, notice how much easier it is." | "כשתיישמו את זה מחר, שימו לב כמה זה קל יותר." Words like "כבר", "עוד", "שוב", "מתי", "אחרי ש" carry presuppositions. | MODERATE for presupposition and leading-question effects in general linguistics and psychology (see Gaps). NO EVIDENCE that it is "hypnotic". |
| Embedded command | A command phrase inside a longer sentence, often marked by a vocal shift | "I don't know how soon you'll *start feeling confident*." | Hebrew conjugates verbs for gender and number, so for a mixed audience use the plural ("תתחילו להרגיש") or the infinitive ("להתחיל להרגיש בטוחים"). | NO EVIDENCE |
| Analog marking | Marking the embedded words nonverbally (pause, lower pitch, gesture) | Slowing down and lowering the voice on "*take action now*" | The same in Hebrew. Stress and pausing carry over directly. | NO EVIDENCE for covert effects. Emphasis and pausing do aid comprehension and memorability (general rhetoric). |
| Truism | A statement that cannot be denied, used to build agreement | "Every one of us has had a moment we wished we'd spoken up." | "לכל אחד מאיתנו היה רגע שבו רצה לומר משהו ושתק." | WEAK. It overlaps with the rapport and "common ground" principle. |
| Yes-set | Three or so undeniable statements, then the target proposition | "You came here tonight. You chose to sit in this room. You want something to change. So let's change it." | "באתם הערב. בחרתם לשבת כאן. אתם רוצים שמשהו ישתנה. אז בואו נשנה." | WEAK. The related "foot-in-the-door" research is behavioural, not verbal agreement, and has small effects. |
| Double bind (illusory choice) | Offering choices that all lead to the desired outcome | "Would you like to start the exercise now, or in a minute?" | "תרצו להתחיל עכשיו, או בעוד דקה?" | NO EVIDENCE as a hypnotic device. It is an ethical concern when the choice is false. |
| Nominalization | Turning a process into an abstract noun, which leaves the listener to fill in meaning | "This is about growth, freedom, and understanding." | "זה עניין של צמיחה, חירות והבנה." Hebrew nominal forms ("ההתחדשות", "ההשתנות") work the same way. | NO EVIDENCE as hypnotic. Vagueness can widen identification ("Barnum-like"). |
| Pacing and leading | Matching the listener's current experience (pacing), then guiding toward a new state (leading) | "You're sitting here, hearing my voice, perhaps thinking about your week... and as you do, you can begin to imagine next year." | "אתם יושבים כאן, שומעים את קולי, אולי חושבים על השבוע שלכם... ותוך כדי, אפשר להתחיל לדמיין את השנה הבאה." | WEAK. Pacing resembles the "common ground" and rapport principles. Nonverbal mimicry research is mixed. |
| Tag question | A sentence ending that invites agreement | "That's something we can all do, isn't it?" | "נכון?", "לא?", "הלא כן?", "ככה זה, לא?" | WEAK |
| Nested loops | Opening story A, then B, then C, and closing them in reverse order (C, B, A), with the key message in the innermost story | A keynote that opens a personal story, breaks to a historical anecdote, delivers the core point, then closes both | Works the same in Hebrew | WEAK. It overlaps with the "open loop" and suspense principles. Evidence is indirect, through narrative-transportation work (Section 4). |
| Generic "you" / second person | Addressing listeners directly, or using "you" to state general truths | "You know that feeling when..." | Hebrew uses generic masculine "אתה" in speech ("אתה יודע איך זה..."). Consider the plural "אתם" or the inclusive "אנחנו" for a mixed audience. | MODERATE (Orvell et al., Section 4) |

### Inferences
- Present the Milton Model to students as a style toolkit (rapport, vagueness that invites identification, rhythm, stories). Do not present it as a covert control system. The patterns that hold up overlap with mainstream rhetoric and psycholinguistics.
- Hebrew's gendered verb morphology makes classic embedded commands awkward for mixed audiences. Plural forms, infinitives and "אנחנו" are practical workarounds.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed controlled experiment on embedded commands or analog marking was located.
- Presupposition research (for example, Loftus's eyewitness leading-question studies) was not fetched in this session. It is a likely source to add for the "MODERATE" grade on presupposition.

## 3. Scientific status of hypnosis, suggestibility and NLP

### Takeaway
Hypnosis is a real, measurable phenomenon. Responsiveness varies stably across individuals: about 10–15% are "highs". It has documented neural correlates and moderate-to-strong clinical efficacy for some conditions, mainly procedural pain. However, (a) indirect or Ericksonian wording is **not** shown to be more effective than direct wording, (b) expectancy is a major mechanism, and (c) NLP and "covert conversational hypnosis" lack empirical support.

### Cited Findings
**Hypnotizability**
- On standardised scales (HGSHS:A and SHSS:C, both 0–12), 10–15% of people pass all or most items and count as "highs" (Woody & Barnier, 2008). — [Barnier et al., Goldsmiths eprint](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/31152/8/1-s2.0-S1053810022000010-main.pdf)
- Fewer than 10% are "virtuosos", scoring 10–12 on SHSS:C. SHSS:C is considered the gold standard. One reported sample had a mean of 5.19 (SD 3.09). — [Kihlstrom, Assessment of Hypnotizability slides (Berkeley)](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/%7Ejfkihlstrom/PDFfiles/SCEH2013ResearchWorkshop/Kihlstrom%20Presentations/AssessmentOfHypnotizability.pdf); [Kihlstrom 2016 encyclopedia entry](https://ocf.berkeley.edu/~jfkihlstrom/PDFs/2010s/2016/Kihlstrom_Hypnosis_EPID2016.pdf)

**Neuroscience**
- Jiang, White, Greicius, Waelde & Spiegel (Stanford), *Cerebral Cortex* (2016; doi:10.1093/cercor/bhw220), compared 36 highly and 21 low-hypnotizable people. Only in highs, and only during hypnosis, they found reduced dorsal anterior cingulate activity, increased connectivity between the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the insula, and reduced connectivity between the DLPFC and the posterior cingulate (default mode network). It was described as the first study of its kind, so replication remains open. — [Stanford Medicine news release](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2016/07/study-identifies-brain-areas-altered-during-hypnotic-trances.html); [STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2016/07/28/hypnosis-psychiatry-brain-activity/)
- Raz, Kirsch, Pollard & Nitkin-Kaner, "Suggestion reduces the Stroop effect," *Psychological Science* (2006): suggestion reduced Stroop interference in highly suggestible people. Raz linked this to frontal-parietal and anterior cingulate attention networks. — [APA Monitor, Mar 2006](https://www.apa.org/monitor/mar06/lab)
- Terhune, Cleeremans, Raz & Lynn, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* (2017), frame hypnosis as top-down regulation in which verbal suggestions shape experience. — [Goldsmiths eprint](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/19831/1/terhune%20et%20al.%20in%20press%20NBR.pdf)
- Suggestion effects on the Stroop task can occur without a formal hypnotic induction. Imaginative suggestion is enough in highly suggestible people, but replication is mixed. — [Suggestion, cognition and behaviour review (hypnosisandsuggestion.org)](https://hypnosisandsuggestion.org/review-article-suggestion,-cognition-and-behaviour.html)

**Expectancy (Kirsch)**
- Kirsch's response-expectancy theory, first set out in *American Psychologist* (1985), holds that hypnotic and placebo effects share a mechanism: response expectancy. The "strong" version (expectancy is the sole cause) has been challenged, and the "weak" version (one major contributor) is widely accepted. — [Lynn/Kirsch reference listing](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/00029157.2007.10401595); [Anales de Psicología review](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/download/31161/30321/143611)

**Direct vs indirect (Ericksonian) suggestion**
- A conceptual and methodological review in *IJCEH* (1993) concluded that suggestion wording has little effect on objective responses to hypnotic test items, that the best-controlled pain studies do not show indirect suggestions to be superior, and that evidence for an interaction with hypnotizability is insufficient. — [Scilit record: "Direct Versus Indirect Suggestions: A Conceptual and Methodological Review"](https://scilit.com/publications/61ec1e9751df71f511cce308d5b91850); [Scite summary](https://scite.ai/authors/victor-neufeld-aKYp)
- One experiment found that direct wording produced *more* felt involuntariness and suggested effects than indirect wording, while behavioural responses did not differ. — [Scite summary](https://scite.ai/authors/victor-neufeld-aKYp)
- De Benedittis (2024, Italian review) reached the same view: the evidence does not support the absolute superiority of indirect methods. — [FrancoAngeli, "Direct vs Indirect Hypnotic Techniques: What to Choose?"](https://francoangeli.it/riviste/articolo/76012)

**Clinical efficacy of hypnosis overall**
- Rosendahl, Alldredge & Haddenhorst (2024, *Frontiers in Psychology*) reviewed 49 meta-analyses (261 distinct primary studies) and rated only 9 as high quality. The strongest evidence is for patients undergoing medical procedures or surgery (12 reviews, 79 studies) and for pain (4 reviews, 65 studies). Effect sizes against controls ranged from d = −0.04 to d = 2.72. — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1330238/full); [PubMed 38268815](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38268815/)

**NLP**
- Witkowski (2010), *Polish Psychological Bulletin* 41(2) (doi:10.2478/v10059-010-0008-0), reviewed the NLP Research Data Base (315 entries) and selected 63 studies in ISI-listed journals. Only a minority supported NLP claims: secondary sources quote 18.2% of 33 studies for one subset. He concluded NLP is pseudoscientific. — [Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/400885498/35-Years-of-NLP-Research); [Donald Clark summary](https://donaldclarkplanb.blogspot.com/2010/11/nlp-scientific-paper-suggests-its.html)
- Sturt et al. (2012), *British Journal of General Practice* 62(604): e757–e764: a Cochrane-style systematic review of 10 studies (5 RCTs, n = 22–106). Four of the five RCTs found no between-group difference, and risk of bias was high or uncertain throughout. Conclusion: "little evidence that NLP interventions improve health-related outcomes". — [Warwick repository](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/49754); [King's College London press release](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/archive/news/nmpc/2012/little-evidence-for-nlp-in-healthcare)

**Israel**
- Israel's Use of Hypnosis Law (1984) is one of the strictest in the world. It restricts hypnosis to licensed doctors, dentists and clinical psychologists and to therapeutic purposes. A Health Ministry Advisory Committee on Hypnosis was set up in 1984. Critics, including mentalist Ben Cale, call the definition of hypnosis vague and too broad. — [Jerusalem Post, "Why is Israel's hypnosis law so strict?"](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/why-is-israels-hypnosis-law-so-strict-677833); [CESNUR / relbib article, "Trance, Meditation and Brainwashing"](https://relbib.de/Record/1724520539)
- The Israel Society of Hypnosis supports the law as protection against charlatans. Its chairman, Dr. Tzahi Arnon, is quoted in the Jerusalem Post. — [Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/why-is-israels-hypnosis-law-so-strict-677833)
- Israeli researchers Lichtenberg, Shapira, Kalish & Abramowitz produced a Hebrew adaptation of, and Israeli norms for, the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scale (Herzog Hospital, Wolfson Medical Center, IDF Mental Health). — [Tel Aviv University Coller research paper listing](https://en-coller.tau.ac.il/sites/coller-english.tau.ac.il/files/RP_131_Kalish.pdf)
- Joseph Meyerson, a past president of the Israeli Society of Hypnosis, chairs the Ministry of Health examination committee for hypnosis accreditation and has taught at Tel Aviv University. — [academia.edu profile](https://biu.academia.edu/JosephMeyerson)
- Moris Kleinhauz co-authored Israeli papers on misuses of hypnosis, dental hypnosis and the gag reflex. — [IJCEH-related listing](https://ijceh.com/hypnosis/hypnotic-induction-profile)

### Inferences
- **Legal note for the course (Israel):** because the 1984 law's definition of hypnosis is broad, a public-speaking course should avoid marketing itself as teaching "hypnosis" or "inducing trance" in audiences. Frame it as "hypnotic-style language" or "Ericksonian rhetoric". Students should take legal advice if they plan demonstrations. This is an inference from the JPost and CESNUR coverage, not legal advice.
- The science supports teaching **expectancy** ("frame what the audience will experience") and **absorption** more than any particular "hypnotic" wording.
- Grades: hypnosis as a clinical tool for procedural pain is **STRONG to MODERATE**. Superiority of indirect over direct suggestion: **NO EVIDENCE / disconfirmed**. NLP and covert conversational hypnosis: **NO EVIDENCE** (reviews negative). Expectancy effects: **STRONG**.

### Gaps
- Steven Jay Lynn's socio-cognitive papers and Kirsch's 1985 article were not fetched directly.
- No current (2025–2026) Israeli hypnosis research publications were found. The Israel Society of Hypnosis website did not surface in search.
- Whether the 1984 law has been amended by November 2026 could not be confirmed. JPost coverage mentions proposed Justice Ministry amendments.

## 4. What IS well supported and overlaps: expectancy, priming, rhythm and repetition, imagery, "you" language, narrative transportation, audience "trance"

### Takeaway
The robust mechanisms behind "hypnotic" speaking are ordinary ones. They are expectancy, narrative transportation (absorption in story), vivid imagery, direct "you" language, and rhythmic structures such as three-part lists and contrasts. These have meaningful but bounded support. Behavioural priming claims should be treated with replication caution.

### Cited Findings
- **Narrative transportation (STRONG):** van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels (2014), *Journal of Consumer Research* 40(5): 797–817, meta-analysed 132 effect sizes from 76 articles. Story features such as an imaginable plot, identifiable characters and verisimilitude increase transportation. Transportation raises affective response, belief, attitude and intention, and lowers critical thought. — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/673383)
- **Generic "you" (MODERATE):** Orvell, Kross & Gelman (2017), *Science* 355(6331): 1299. Across six experiments, generic "you" expressed norms and helped people "normalize" and make meaning of negative experiences, creating psychological distance. In a meaning-making condition, 46% used generic "you" at least once, against 10% (relive) and 3% (neutral). — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170323141411.htm); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/539234)
- A separate later PNAS paper by the same group links generic "you" to resonance with readers (Kindle highlighting). It is distinct from the *Science* study. — [Univ. of Michigan, "The linguistic device that creates resonance..."](https://prod.lsa.umich.edu/psych/news-events/all-news/faculty-news/the-linguistic-device-that-creates-resonance-between-people-and-.html)
- **Rhythm and rhetorical "claptraps" (MODERATE):** Atkinson (*Our Masters' Voices*, 1984) showed that three-part lists and contrasts let audiences predict completion points and cue applause. He called contrast the most frequent applause device. — [The Psychologist (BPS)](https://bps.org.uk/psychologist/leader-speeches-2005-party-political-conferences); [NCA Communication Currents](https://www.natcom.org/communication-currents/engaging-audiences-through-rhetorical-devices)
- Bull (2016) found only a mean 61% of applause fully synchronised with speech. He argued Atkinson's theory must distinguish invited from uninvited applause. — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/100468/3/A_PublishedVersion.pdf); [Emotionality in audience responses, RUDN journal](https://journals.rudn.ru/linguistics/article/view/27475)
- **Expectancy (STRONG):** expectancy is a shared mechanism of hypnotic and placebo responses (Kirsch). — [Anales de Psicología review](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/download/31161/30321/143611)
- **Absorption / neural "trance" (MODERATE, but only in highs):** the Stanford fMRI findings of reduced dACC activity (absorption, less monitoring) apply only to highly hypnotizable people during formal hypnosis. — [Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2016/07/study-identifies-brain-areas-altered-during-hypnotic-trances.html)

### Inferences
- Speakers can legitimately aim for "absorption" by using stories with an imaginable plot, identifiable characters and sensory detail. This is the best-supported route to the felt "trance-like" quality audiences report.
- Generic "you" statements ("you know that moment when...") are a research-backed way to make personal stories feel universal. In Hebrew the colloquial generic "אתה" plays the same role. Mixed-gender phrasing ("אתם", "אנחנו", "כל אחד מאיתנו") avoids excluding listeners.
- Rhythm devices (lists of three, contrast) reliably structure audience response. Teach them as tools for clarity and memorability, and present them as cues for applause rather than a mechanism of hypnosis.
- Priming: behavioural priming (for example, the "elderly-walking" effect) has had high-profile replication failures. Teach semantic and framing effects (Section 5) instead of claims about subliminal priming.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found that measures trance-like states in audiences of rhythmic oratory or preaching cadence. Claims about "preaching trance" remain anecdotal or ethnographic in this research pass.
- Specific replication-failure citations for behavioural priming (for example, Doyen et al. 2012) were not fetched in this session.

## 5. Metaphor research: Lakoff & Johnson, Thibodeau & Boroditsky, Sopory & Dillard, Charteris-Black, therapeutic metaphor, and how to build metaphors

### Takeaway
Metaphors frame reasoning, and the effect is real but small and context-dependent. Sopory & Dillard's meta-analysis shows a modest persuasive edge for metaphor over literal language, best explained by metaphor organising the message ("superior organization"). The crime beast/virus effect replicates only partially and depends on prior attitudes and on how the metaphor is extended. A few well-chosen, coherent, extended metaphors beat many mixed ones.

### Cited Findings
- **Thibodeau & Boroditsky (2011),** "Metaphors We Think With: The Role of Metaphor in Reasoning," *PLoS ONE* 6(2): e16782 (doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0016782). Framing crime as a "beast" made people more likely to propose enforcement, and framing it as a "virus" made them more likely to propose social reform. Secondary reports cite 71% vs 54% enforcement for a single-word metaphor and about 74% for the extended beast version. The effect held across political affiliation. Only about 3% of participants named the metaphor as influential. Most credited the crime statistics. — [Nature News 2011](https://www.nature.com/news/2011/110223/full/news.2011.115.html); [Discover Magazine](https://www.discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/is-crime-a-virus-or-a-beast-how-metaphors-shape-our-thoughts-and-decisions); [paper PDF (Heidelberg course copy)](https://www.cl.uni-heidelberg.de/courses/ss19/FLR/material/thibodeau.pdf)
- Exact percentages differ across secondary reports (71% vs 74%). Verify against the original. — [Wylie Communications](https://www.wyliecomm.com/2021/04/how-metaphors-work-to-persuade)
- **Replications:** Reijnierse, Burgers, Krennmayr & Steen (2015), *Metaphor and the Social World*, concluded that metaphorical framing effects "may be more subtle than has been assumed". — [UvA DARE record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/ebcaa63f-8e58-402a-90a7-4883e3d68f2e). A German replication (Christmann & Göhring, 2016, *Scientific Data*) noted that the beast metaphor is unusual in German, which raises cultural and linguistic limits. — [PMC5079120](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5079120/). A larger follow-up by Thibodeau and colleagues found the framing effect appears once prior attitudes toward crime are controlled for. — [PMC4517745](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517745)
- A later meta-analysis, "Metaphorical framing in political discourse through words vs concepts" (Brugman, Burgers & Vis, 2019, *Language and Cognition*), examined the size of metaphorical framing effects. Its pooled figures could not be fetched here. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/865DFAB51172998E1C9574D74E275AAE)
- **Sopory & Dillard (2002),** "The Persuasive Effects of Metaphor: A Meta-Analysis," *Human Communication Research* 28(3): 382–419. Of six theories, the "superior organization" explanation was best supported. — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-persuasive-effects-of-metaphor-a-meta-analysis/). Secondary sources summarise the advantage as about a 6% persuasion bump over literal messages, and report that fewer metaphors produced more attitude change than many. Treat both as secondary reports and verify. — [Medium, Bending the Arc](https://medium.com/bending-the-arc/metaphors-in-persuasive-communications-6d714849f403); [Wylie Communications (gives an incorrect 2006 date)](https://www.wyliecomm.com/?p=33484)
- **Charteris-Black,** *Politicians and Rhetoric: The Persuasive Power of Metaphor* (Palgrave, 2005; 2nd ed. 2011). The book analyses how leaders use systematic metaphor to build political myths of monsters, villains and heroic leaders: King's messianic myth, Thatcher as Boudicca, Reagan's romantic myth, Obama's American Dream. The 2nd edition added Powell, Reagan and Obama. Charteris-Black's term "purposeful metaphor" is discussed in a 2012 article. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/9780230319899); [UWE repository](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/1054267); [Exploring Geopolitics interview](https://exploringgeopolitics.org/jonathan-charteris-black-politicians-and-rhetoric-the-persuasive-power-of-metaphor/)
- **Conceptual metaphor theory:** Lakoff & Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) underlies this work. It holds that abstract thought is structured by metaphors such as ARGUMENT IS WAR and TIME IS MONEY. The 2011 Thibodeau & Boroditsky study is framed as a test of it. — [Clean Language summary](https://cleanlanguage.com/metaphors-we-think-with/)

**How to build a powerful metaphor (practical synthesis):**
1. **Choose the source domain for its inferences.** "Crime is a virus" implies diagnosis, prevention and treatment. "Crime is a beast" implies capture and caging. Pick the metaphor whose logic leads where your argument goes.
2. **Use one coherent master metaphor**, introduced early and extended through the speech. The organization account and the "fewer is better" result both argue against mixing.
3. **Make it sensory and concrete.** Imaginability drives transportation (van Laer 2014).
4. **Match it to the culture and language.** The German replication shows metaphors carry differently across languages. Hebrew-rooted images ("לבנות חומה", "מדבר", "יציאה ממצרים", "זרעים שנזרעים בדמעה") draw on shared biblical and Zionist frames.
5. **Pre-test it**, for example by asking listeners what the metaphor implies, since people rarely notice metaphors' influence (3% in Thibodeau & Boroditsky).

**Hebrew illustration (constructed):** "הכלכלה היא לא מכונה שמתקלקלת ומתקנים. היא גן: צריך להשקות, לגזום, ולחכות לעונה." Framing the economy as a garden (patience, cultivation) instead of a machine (repair, control) points listeners toward a long-term policy argument.

### Inferences
- Grades: metaphorical framing affects reasoning: **MODERATE** (real, small, context-dependent). Metaphor is more persuasive than literal language: **MODERATE**, with a small average effect. Extended over single metaphor: **WEAK/mixed**. Therapeutic metaphor as a stand-alone clinical technique: **WEAK** (the support is mostly indirect, through narrative research).
- Because audiences rarely notice metaphors, they are powerful and also ethically loaded (Section 6).

### Gaps
- David Gordon's *Therapeutic Metaphors* (1978) and Richard Kopp's *Metaphor Therapy* (1995) are the standard texts. No primary pages or empirical tests of their methods were retrieved.
- Exact pooled effect sizes (r) for Sopory & Dillard and for the 2019 framing meta-analysis were not verified, because cambridge.org was blocked.
- No Hebrew-language replication of the beast/virus framing study was found.

## 6. Ethics: where influence becomes manipulation; responsible use

### Takeaway
The techniques with the most research support (metaphor framing, narrative transportation) work partly because audiences don't notice them, which is why ethics matter. Manipulation begins when a speaker exploits that lack of awareness to bypass evaluation, serves their own interest against the audience's, or creates false choices. Israel adds a legal layer: hypnosis itself is regulated.

### Cited Findings
- Participants rarely recognise a metaphor's influence (about 3% credited it), which shows how framing can work below awareness. — [Discover Magazine](https://www.discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/is-crime-a-virus-or-a-beast-how-metaphors-shape-our-thoughts-and-decisions)
- Narrative transportation reduces critical thought. — [van Laer et al. 2014, City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/)
- Charteris-Black documents how political metaphor builds myths of "monsters, villains and heroic leaders", including in demagogic rhetoric such as Powell's. — [UWE repository](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/1054267)
- Israel restricts hypnosis to licensed professionals for therapy. The Israel Society of Hypnosis defends the law as protecting the public from "charlatans and irresponsible therapists". — [Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/why-is-israels-hypnosis-law-so-strict-677833)
- The CESNUR analysis links the law's broad definition to fears about "brainwashing" by new religious movements. — [relbib / CESNUR](https://relbib.de/Record/1724520539)

### Inferences
Proposed ethical tests for the course, synthesised by the researcher:
1. **Transparency test:** would the technique still work if you explained it afterwards? Ethical framing usually survives disclosure ("I used a garden metaphor because I believe patience matters here").
2. **Interest test:** does the change serve the listener's own goals, or only the speaker's?
3. **Autonomy test:** are real choices offered? Avoid false double binds in sales or political settings.
4. **Truth test:** metaphors and stories must not misrepresent facts. Thibodeau and Boroditsky's participants believed they were reasoning from the statistics.
5. **Vulnerability test:** take extra care with highly suggestible people, people in distress, and captive audiences. Avoid confusion techniques on stage.
6. **Legal test (Israel):** do not stage hypnotic inductions or call speeches "hypnosis" without the licensing the 1984 law requires.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed ethics framework specific to "conversational hypnosis" in public speaking was found. General persuasion-ethics literature was not retrieved in this pass.

## 7. Concrete practice exercises (synthesised from the above)

### Takeaway
Exercises should train the evidence-backed skills (story absorption, metaphor choice, rhythm, "you" language, expectancy framing) and treat Milton Model patterns as stylistic drills, with an ethics debrief after each.

### Cited Findings
- Story features that drive transportation are an imaginable plot, identifiable characters and verisimilitude. — [van Laer et al. 2014](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/)
- Three-part lists and contrasts structure audience response. — [The Psychologist (BPS)](https://bps.org.uk/psychologist/leader-speeches-2005-party-political-conferences)
- Generic "you" helps listeners normalise experience and draw meaning from it. — [ScienceDaily (Orvell et al.)](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170323141411.htm)
- Metaphor choice shifts preferred solutions, and audiences rarely notice. — [Nature News](https://www.nature.com/news/2011/110223/full/news.2011.115.html)

### Inferences (exercise set)
1. **Beast/virus lab (metaphor framing).** Students write the same 100-word problem statement twice, with two opposing master metaphors (for example, Hebrew "התייקרות המחיה היא מגפה" vs "היא חיה טורפת"). Peers list the solutions each version implies. Debrief on the inferences each metaphor carries.
2. **Master metaphor extension.** Pick one source domain (journey, garden, building, sea) and extend it through intro, body and close without mixing. Peers flag any mixed metaphor.
3. **Transportation story drill.** Tell a two-minute story with one identifiable character, a clear plot and at least three sensory details. Peers rate absorption (did they "forget the room"?) on a 1–7 scale, adapted from transportation scales.
4. **Generic "you" conversion.** Rewrite a personal "I" story so that its lesson is stated with generic "you" or "כל אחד מאיתנו". Compare how relatable each version feels.
5. **Rhythm workshop.** Convert three key points into (a) a list of three and (b) a contrast pair. Deliver them with pauses and note where the audience reacts.
6. **Milton pattern drills (style only).** Write one sentence each for presupposition, truism, yes-set, tag question, permissive suggestion and pacing-and-leading on your speech topic, in English and Hebrew, with plural forms for mixed audiences. Then rewrite each as a direct statement and discuss which feels more honest and which more persuasive.
7. **Utilization improv.** The facilitator introduces disruptions (a phone rings, a skeptical question, a power cut). The speaker must fold each one into the message within ten seconds.
8. **Nested-loop keynote.** Build a five-minute talk as open story A, open story B, core message, close B, close A. Check audience recall of the core message.
9. **Expectancy framing.** Open with a frame for the experience ("By the end of these ten minutes, you'll have one tool you can use tonight"), then deliver on it. Discuss promise versus manipulation.
10. **Ethics debrief.** After each exercise, run the transparency, interest, autonomy and truth tests from Section 6.

### Gaps
- None of these exercises has been validated in controlled studies of speaking courses. They are the researcher's synthesis from the research above.
